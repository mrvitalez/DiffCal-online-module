1.4The Tangent and Velocity Problems

Before calculus, mathematics was great at dealing with averages: the average speed of a trip, the slope of a straight line, and so on. But then questions like "What is the speed exactly at this instant?" or "What is the slope of the curve at this exact point?" came up, and they needed answers. Both questions seem impossible to answer at first, but they led to the creation of the derivative. In this section we look at both, and we will see that they are really the same problem.

The tangent problem. In geometry we defined a tangent as the line that touches a circle exactly once. However, this definition does not capture what a tangent really is for other curves. Picture a curve \(C\) with a steep line \(l\) that crosses it exactly once, but clearly cuts through it and does not look like a tangent at all; and a line \(t\) that just grazes \(C\) at a point but meets the curve again somewhere else. "Touches once" is not the right idea.

So, if we want the slope of the tangent line at a point \(P(a, f(a))\) on a curve \(y = f(x)\), how do we do it? Again, we know that a line's slope is easy: it is just rise over run. A curve, however, does not have a single slope; its steepness keeps changing. The trick is to take a second point \(Q(x, f(x))\) on the curve and draw the secant line \(PQ\), a line that meets the curve at two points. Its slope is

Key equations
\[ m_{PQ} = \frac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a} \qquad (1) \]

Now let \(Q\) slide along the curve toward \(P\). The secant lines turn and settle down on one line, the tangent line. So the slope of the tangent is the limit of the slopes of the secant lines:

Key equations
\[ m = \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a} \qquad (2) \]

This limit, if it exists, is called the derivative of \(f\) at \(a\), and it is the whole of Module 3. Take note that we cannot just put \(x = a\) into (2): we would get \(0/0\). We must simplify first, and only then let \(x\) approach \(a\). Worked Examples 2.1 and 2.2 show how.

Exercise Find the equation of the tangent line to \(y = x^3\) at \(P(2, 8)\). Hint: \(x^3 - 8 = (x - 2)(x^2 + 2x + 4)\). Answer: \(m = 12\), so \(y = 12x - 16\).

The velocity problem. In our physics class we defined the average velocity as the ratio of the change in position to the change in time. If \(s(t)\) is the position at time \(t\), then over the time interval from \(t\) to \(t + h\),

Key equations
\[ v_{\text{ave}} = \frac{\Delta s}{\Delta t} = \frac{s(t + h) - s(t)}{h} \qquad (3) \]

This tells us how fast the object moves on average, not at a precise moment. So, to find the instantaneous velocity, we shrink the time interval toward zero. That is basically the limit:

Key equations
\[ v(t) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{s(t + h) - s(t)}{h} \qquad (4) \]

If this limit exists, it is the instantaneous velocity. Notice that (4) has exactly the same form as (2): the velocity is the slope of the tangent line to the position graph. One idea, two problems.

Exercise A ball is thrown upward at 40 ft/s, and its height after \(t\) seconds is \(y = 40t - 16t^2\). Estimate the velocity at \(t = 2\) using \(h = \pm 0.1\). Answer: the two average velocities are \(-25.6\) and \(-22.4\) ft/s, and the limit is \(v(2) = -24\) ft/s; the negative sign tells us the ball is already coming down.
Common mistake Substituting \(h = 0\) (or \(x = a\)) right away in (2) or (4). That always gives \(0/0\), which is not an answer; it is a signal that we must simplify first.

1.5The Limit of a Function

From the tangent and velocity problems, we can build an intuitive definition of a limit. This topic answers the question "What value does a function approach as \(x\) gets close to a number?"

Definition We write \(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L\) and say "the limit of \(f(x)\), as \(x\) approaches \(a\), equals \(L\)" if we can make the values of \(f(x)\) as close to \(L\) as we like by taking \(x\) close enough to \(a\) (on either side of \(a\)) but not equal to \(a\).

An alternative notation is \(f(x) \to L\) as \(x \to a\). Notice that we did not say \(x = a\) but rather \(x \to a\), because what we need is how \(f\) behaves near \(a\). The value \(f(a)\) itself does not matter; \(f\) does not even have to be defined at \(a\).

Guessing a limit from a table. Let's guess \(\lim_{x \to 1} \dfrac{x - 1}{x^2 - 1}\). At \(x = 1\) the formula gives \(0/0\), so let's try values near 1:

\(x < 1\)\(f(x)\)\(x > 1\)\(f(x)\)

0.90.5263161.10.476190

We can see that as \(x\) gets closer to 1 from both sides, \(f(x)\) gets closer to 0.5. We can confirm it with algebra: \(\dfrac{x - 1}{(x - 1)(x + 1)} = \dfrac{1}{x + 1}\) for \(x \ne 1\), and \(\dfrac{1}{1 + 1} = \dfrac12\).

Common mistake Trusting a calculator too far. For \(\dfrac{\sqrt{t^2 + 9} - 3}{t^2}\), the table gives 0.166666… for \(t = 0.01\) and \(t = 0.001\), but for \(t = 0.00000001\) many calculators show 0! Does that mean the limit is 0? No. Do you know why? \(\sqrt{t^2 + 9}\) is so close to 3 that the calculator rounds the numerator to 0. The true limit is \(\tfrac16\) (Example 2.4). Tables suggest; algebra proves.

One-sided limits. Sometimes \(f\) approaches different values from the two sides. We write \(\lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = L\) for the left-hand limit (we only take \(x < a\)) and \(\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = L\) for the right-hand limit (only \(x > a\)). In the graph below, as \(x \to 2^-\) the values go to 3, but as \(x \to 2^+\) they go to 1.

Theorem \(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L\) if and only if \(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = L\) and \(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = L\). So in the graph above, \(\lim_{x \to 2} f(x)\) does not exist.

Infinite limits. What about \(\lim_{x \to 0} \dfrac{1}{x^2}\)? As \(x\) gets closer to 0, \(1/x^2\) becomes very large: 1, 4, 25, 100, 400, 10 000, 1 000 000 for \(x = 1, 0.5, 0.2, 0.1, 0.05, 0.01, 0.001\). The values do not approach a number, but they grow without bound, and we write \(\lim_{x \to 0} \dfrac{1}{x^2} = \infty\). Similarly \(\lim_{x \to 0} \left(-\dfrac{1}{x^2}\right) = -\infty\). Take note that \(\infty\) is not a number; writing "= ∞" is just a way of saying how the limit fails to exist.

The same idea works one side at a time, \(\lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = \pm\infty\) and \(\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = \pm\infty\), remembering that \(x \to a^-\) means we only use \(x < a\). If any of these happens, the line \(x = a\) is a vertical asymptote of the graph.

Key equations
\[ \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L \iff \lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = L = \lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) \]

1.6Calculating Limits Using the Limit Laws

Tables and graphs only suggest limits, and as we saw, a calculator can even lie to us. So, how do we compute limits exactly? We use the Limit Laws. Suppose \(\lim_{x \to a} f(x)\) and \(\lim_{x \to a} g(x)\) both exist, and \(c\) is a constant. Then the limit of a sum is the sum of the limits, the limit of a product is the product of the limits, and so on:

Key equations
\[ \lim [f(x) \pm g(x)] = \lim f(x) \pm \lim g(x), \qquad \lim [c f(x)] = c \lim f(x) \]
\[ \lim [f(x) g(x)] = \lim f(x) \cdot \lim g(x), \qquad \lim \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{\lim f(x)}{\lim g(x)} \;\; (\lim g(x) \ne 0) \]
\[ \lim [f(x)]^n = [\lim f(x)]^n, \qquad \lim \sqrt[n]{f(x)} = \sqrt[n]{\lim f(x)} \]

Direct substitution. Putting these laws together gives a very useful shortcut: if \(f\) is a polynomial or a rational function and \(a\) is in its domain, then \(\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)\). For example, \(\lim_{x \to 2} (x^3 - 4x + 1) = 8 - 8 + 1 = 1\).

When substitution gives 0/0. This is the interesting case, and it is exactly what happens with every tangent and velocity problem. The rule is: simplify first, then substitute. Three tools cover most cases:

  • Factor and cancel. \(\dfrac{x^2 - 9}{x - 3} = \dfrac{(x - 3)(x + 3)}{x - 3} = x + 3\) for \(x \ne 3\), so the limit as \(x \to 3\) is 6. We are allowed to cancel because a limit never uses \(x = a\) itself.
  • Multiply by the conjugate when there is a square root, as in Example 2.2 and Example 2.4.
  • Combine fractions when there is a fraction inside a fraction.

The Squeeze Theorem. Sometimes no algebra helps. If \(g(x) \le f(x) \le h(x)\) near \(a\) and \(\lim g(x) = \lim h(x) = L\), then \(\lim f(x) = L\) too: \(f\) is trapped between two functions going to the same place. Recall that \(-1 \le \cos\theta \le 1\) for every \(\theta\); multiplying by \(x^2 \ge 0\) gives \(-x^2 \le x^2\cos(1/x) \le x^2\), and both sides go to 0.

Common mistake Using the Product Law on \(\lim_{x \to 0} x^2 \cos(1/x)\). We would need \(\lim_{x \to 0} \cos(1/x)\), which does not exist (it keeps oscillating). The Limit Laws only apply when each limit exists; here we need the Squeeze Theorem.

1.7The Precise Definition of a Limit

"As close as we like" and "close enough" are good for intuition, but they are not precise. Mathematicians made them precise with two Greek letters.

Definition Let \(f\) be defined on an open interval containing \(a\), except possibly at \(a\). Then \(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L\) if for every number \(\varepsilon > 0\) there is a number \(\delta > 0\) such that if \(0 < |x - a| < \delta\) then \(|f(x) - L| < \varepsilon\).

What does this actually mean? The epsilon (\(\varepsilon\)) represents how close you want \(f(x)\) to be to \(L\). Think of it as how close the output must be. On the other hand, the delta (\(\delta\)) represents how close \(x\) must be to \(a\), or how close the input must be. The definition is like a challenge: someone gives us any tolerance \(\varepsilon\), no matter how tiny, and we must always be able to answer with a \(\delta\) that keeps the outputs within that tolerance.

In a picture: draw the horizontal band \(L - \varepsilon < y < L + \varepsilon\). We must find a vertical band \(a - \delta < x < a + \delta\) such that, inside it (except possibly at \(x = a\)), the graph stays inside the horizontal band.

Example Prove \(\lim_{x \to 3} (4x - 5) = 7\). Scratch work: we want \(|(4x - 5) - 7| = |4x - 12| = 4|x - 3| < \varepsilon\), that is, \(|x - 3| < \varepsilon/4\). So we choose \(\delta = \varepsilon/4\). Proof: given \(\varepsilon > 0\), let \(\delta = \varepsilon/4\). If \(0 < |x - 3| < \delta\), then \(|(4x - 5) - 7| = 4|x - 3| < 4\delta = \varepsilon\). ∎

Notice the pattern: we work backwards from \(|f(x) - L| < \varepsilon\) to find \(\delta\) (the scratch work), and then we write the proof forwards. For a line with slope \(m\), \(\delta = \varepsilon/|m|\) always works.

Common mistake Choosing \(\delta\) that depends on \(x\). The number \(\delta\) may depend on \(\varepsilon\) (and on \(a\)), but never on \(x\): it must work for all \(x\) in the interval at once.

1.8Continuity

For polynomials, finding a limit was as easy as substituting. Functions with that nice property have a name: they are continuous. In physics, most motions are continuous; a planet does not suddenly jump from one point of its orbit to another.

Definition A function \(f\) is continuous at \(a\) if \(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)\). This asks for three things: \(f(a)\) is defined, \(\lim_{x \to a} f(x)\) exists, and the two are equal.

If any of the three fails, \(f\) is discontinuous at \(a\), and we name the kind of break: a removable discontinuity (a hole: the limit exists but \(f(a)\) is missing or wrong, and redefining one value fixes it), a jump discontinuity (the one-sided limits are different), or an infinite discontinuity (a vertical asymptote).

Which functions are continuous? Polynomials, rational functions, root functions and the trigonometric functions are continuous at every number in their domains. Sums, differences, products, quotients (where the denominator is not 0) and composites of continuous functions are continuous too. So most of the time, the only places to check are where a denominator is zero or where a piecewise function changes formula.

Piecewise functions. At the point where the formula changes, we compare the two one-sided limits with the function value. If a constant is unknown, we choose it so that the pieces meet (Example 2.6).

The Intermediate Value Theorem Suppose \(f\) is continuous on the closed interval \([a, b]\) and \(N\) is any number between \(f(a)\) and \(f(b)\). Then there is a number \(c\) in \((a, b)\) with \(f(c) = N\).

In words: a continuous function cannot skip a value. Its most useful form is for roots: if \(f(a)\) and \(f(b)\) have opposite signs, then \(f(c) = 0\) for some \(c\) between them. For example, \(f(x) = x^3 + 2x - 5\) gives \(f(1) = -2 < 0\) and \(f(2) = 7 > 0\), so there is a root between 1 and 2.

Common mistake Forgetting to check continuity before using the IVT. \(f(x) = 1/x\) is \(-1\) at \(x = -1\) and \(1\) at \(x = 1\), yet it is never 0: it is not continuous on \([-1, 1]\).
Key equations
\[ \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a) \quad \text{(continuity at } a\text{)} \]

★Key takeaways

  • The slope of a tangent and an instantaneous velocity are the same limit: m = lim (f(x) − f(a))/(x − a) as x → a.
  • A limit describes f near a, not at a; f(a) does not even have to exist.
  • A two-sided limit exists exactly when both one-sided limits exist and are equal.
  • If substitution gives 0/0, simplify first (factor, multiply by the conjugate, combine fractions), then substitute.
  • A table only suggests a limit and a calculator can round to a wrong value; algebra (or the Squeeze Theorem) proves it.
  • ε is how close the output must be, δ is how close the input must be; δ may depend on ε but never on x.
  • f is continuous at a when lim f(x) = f(a); the IVT then guarantees a root wherever f changes sign.

Example 2.1 The tangent line to a parabola

Find an equation of the tangent line to the parabola \(y = x^2\) at the point \(P(1, 1)\).

(From the instructor's module)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: \(f(x) = x^2\), the point \(P(1, 1)\), so \(a = 1\).   Find: the slope \(m\) of the tangent and the equation of the line.

Plan: We already know one point, \(P\). For the slope, we take a second point \(Q(x, x^2)\) on the parabola, find the secant slope \(m_{PQ}\), and let \(Q\) approach \(P\). Then we use the point-slope form.

Step 1. Write the slope of the secant line through \(P(1, 1)\) and \(Q(x, x^2)\):

\[ m_{PQ} = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1} = \frac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1}. \]

Step 2. Get a feel with numbers. With \(Q(1.5, 2.25)\): \(m_{PQ} = \dfrac{2.25 - 1}{1.5 - 1} = \dfrac{1.25}{0.5} = 2.5\). Trying more values: \(x = 2, 1.5, 1.1, 1.01, 1.001\) give \(3, 2.5, 2.1, 2.01, 2.001\), and \(x = 0, 0.5, 0.9, 0.99, 0.999\) give \(1, 1.5, 1.9, 1.99, 1.999\). It appears the slopes approach 2.

Step 3. Confirm with the limit (2). Substituting \(x = 1\) gives \(0/0\), so we factor the numerator first:

\[ m = \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1} = \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{(x - 1)(x + 1)}{x - 1} = \lim_{x \to 1} (x + 1) = 2. \]

Step 4. Use the point-slope form \(y - y_1 = m(x - x_1)\) with \(P(1, 1)\) and \(m = 2\):

\[ y - 1 = 2(x - 1) \;\Longrightarrow\; y = 2x - 1. \]

Answer: \(\boxed{y = 2x - 1}\). That is our answer.

Check: The line passes through \(P\): \(2(1) - 1 = 1\) ✓. And \(x^2 - (2x - 1) = (x - 1)^2 \ge 0\), so the parabola stays above the line and touches it only at \(x = 1\), just as a tangent should here.

Example 2.2 Tangent line to a square-root curve

Find an equation of the tangent line to the curve \(y = \sqrt{x}\) at the point \(P(4, 2)\).

(From the instructor's module)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: \(f(x) = \sqrt{x}\), \(a = 4\), \(f(4) = \sqrt4 = 2\).   Find: the slope of the tangent and its equation.

Plan: Use formula (2). Substituting \(x = 4\) right away makes the expression collapse into \(0/0\), so we multiply by the conjugate of the numerator to remove the square root from the top.

Step 1. Set up the limit for the slope:

\[ m = \lim_{x \to 4} \frac{f(x) - f(4)}{x - 4} = \lim_{x \to 4} \frac{\sqrt{x} - 2}{x - 4}. \]

Step 2. Multiply the numerator and the denominator by the conjugate \(\sqrt{x} + 2\), and use \((\sqrt{x} - 2)(\sqrt{x} + 2) = x - 4\):

\[ m = \lim_{x \to 4} \frac{\sqrt{x} - 2}{x - 4} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{x} + 2}{\sqrt{x} + 2} = \lim_{x \to 4} \frac{x - 4}{(x - 4)(\sqrt{x} + 2)}. \]

Step 3. Cancel \(x - 4\) (allowed, since \(x \ne 4\) in a limit), then substitute:

\[ m = \lim_{x \to 4} \frac{1}{\sqrt{x} + 2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt4 + 2} = \frac14. \]

Step 4. Point-slope form with \(P(4, 2)\):

\[ y - 2 = \tfrac14 (x - 4) \;\Longrightarrow\; y = \tfrac14 x + 1. \]

Answer: \(\boxed{y = \tfrac14 x + 1}\). So, the equation of the tangent is \(y = \frac14 x + 1\).

Check: Numerically: the secant slope from \(x = 4\) to \(x = 4.01\) is \((\sqrt{4.01} - 2)/0.01 \approx 0.2498\), very close to \(\tfrac14\) ✓.

Example 2.3 Velocity of a falling ball

A ball is dropped from the upper floor of a building, 450 m above the ground. Find the velocity of the ball after 5 seconds. Use \(g = 9.8\) m/s².

(From the instructor's module)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: the ball starts from rest, so the distance fallen is \(s(t) = s_0 + v_0 t + \tfrac12 g t^2 = 4.9t^2\) (m).   Find: the instantaneous velocity \(v(5)\).

Plan: First estimate with an average velocity over a short interval (formula (3)), then compute the exact limit (4).

Step 1. Recall the kinematic equation with \(s_0 = 0\), \(v_0 = 0\): \(s(t) = 4.9t^2\). The ball is still falling at \(t = 5\), since \(s(5) = 122.5\) m is less than 450 m.

Step 2. Estimate with the interval from \(t = 5\) to \(t = 5.1\):

\[ v_{\text{ave}} = \frac{s(5.1) - s(5)}{0.1} = \frac{4.9(5.1)^2 - 4.9(5)^2}{0.1} = \frac{127.449 - 122.5}{0.1} = 49.49 \text{ m/s}. \]

Step 3. Shrink the interval: use \([5, 5 + h]\) and expand \((5 + h)^2 = 25 + 10h + h^2\):

\[ \frac{s(5 + h) - s(5)}{h} = \frac{4.9(25 + 10h + h^2) - 4.9(25)}{h} = \frac{4.9(10h + h^2)}{h} = 4.9(10 + h). \]

Step 4. Take the limit as \(h \to 0\):

\[ v(5) = \lim_{h \to 0} 4.9(10 + h) = 49 \text{ m/s}. \]

Answer: \(\boxed{v(5) = 49 \text{ m/s}}\) downward. That is our answer.

Check: Physics agrees: \(v = gt = 9.8 \times 5 = 49\) m/s ✓. And our estimate 49.49 m/s was slightly larger, because the ball speeds up during \([5, 5.1]\).

Example 2.4 A limit that a calculator gets wrong

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{t \to 0} \frac{\sqrt{t^2 + 9} - 3}{t^2}\).

(From the instructor's module)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: the function \(f(t) = \dfrac{\sqrt{t^2 + 9} - 3}{t^2}\).   Find: its limit as \(t \to 0\).

Plan: If we look intently at this question, we can see a glaring problem: substitution gives \(0/0\). That is a signal to simplify. With a square root in the numerator, we multiply by its conjugate.

Step 1. Multiply the top and bottom by the conjugate \(\sqrt{t^2 + 9} + 3\):

\[ \frac{\sqrt{t^2 + 9} - 3}{t^2} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{t^2 + 9} + 3}{\sqrt{t^2 + 9} + 3} = \frac{(t^2 + 9) - 9}{t^2\left(\sqrt{t^2 + 9} + 3\right)}. \]

Step 2. Simplify the numerator to \(t^2\) and cancel it with the \(t^2\) in the denominator (\(t \ne 0\)):

\[ = \frac{t^2}{t^2\left(\sqrt{t^2 + 9} + 3\right)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{t^2 + 9} + 3}. \]

Step 3. Now substitute \(t = 0\):

\[ \lim_{t \to 0} \frac{1}{\sqrt{t^2 + 9} + 3} = \frac{1}{\sqrt9 + 3} = \frac16. \]

Answer: \(\boxed{\tfrac16}\).

Check: A table gives 0.162277 (t = 1), 0.165525 (0.5), 0.166620 (0.1), 0.166666 (0.01), all approaching 0.1666… = 1/6 ✓. For extremely small \(t\) (like \(10^{-8}\)) a calculator shows 0: it rounds \(\sqrt{t^2 + 9}\) to exactly 3. That is a rounding error, not the limit.

Example 2.5 Infinite one-sided limits

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 3^+} \frac{2x}{x - 3}\) and \(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 3^-} \frac{2x}{x - 3}\). What does this say about the graph?

(From the instructor's module)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: \(f(x) = \dfrac{2x}{x - 3}\).   Find: both one-sided limits at 3.

Plan: Substitution gives \(6/0\): a nonzero number over something going to 0. That means the limit is infinite; we only need the sign of the fraction on each side.

Step 1. Numerator: as \(x \to 3\), \(2x \to 6\), a positive number.

Step 2. Right side, \(x \to 3^+\) (\(x\) slightly bigger than 3, like 3.01): \(x - 3\) is a small positive number, so

\[ \frac{2x}{x - 3} \approx \frac{6}{\text{small } +} \to \infty. \]

Step 3. Left side, \(x \to 3^-\) (like 2.99): \(x - 3\) is a small negative number, so

\[ \frac{2x}{x - 3} \approx \frac{6}{\text{small } -} \to -\infty. \]

Answer: \(\boxed{\lim_{x \to 3^+} \frac{2x}{x - 3} = \infty}\), \(\boxed{\lim_{x \to 3^-} \frac{2x}{x - 3} = -\infty}\); the line \(x = 3\) is a vertical asymptote.

Check: \(f(3.01) = 6.02/0.01 = 602\) and \(f(2.99) = 5.98/(-0.01) = -598\) ✓.

Example 2.6 Making a piecewise function continuous

Find the constant \(k\) that makes \(g(x) = \begin{cases} kx + 3 & x < 1 \\ x^2 + 2k & x \ge 1 \end{cases}\) continuous everywhere.

Show solution (try it first)

Given: two polynomial pieces that meet at \(x = 1\).   Find: the value of \(k\).

Plan: Each piece is a polynomial, so \(g\) is continuous everywhere except possibly at \(x = 1\). There we need the left-hand limit, the right-hand limit and \(g(1)\) to be equal.

Step 1. Left-hand limit (use the piece for \(x < 1\)):

\[ \lim_{x \to 1^-} (kx + 3) = k + 3. \]

Step 2. Right-hand limit and value (use the piece for \(x \ge 1\)):

\[ \lim_{x \to 1^+} (x^2 + 2k) = 1 + 2k = g(1). \]

Step 3. Set them equal and solve for \(k\):

\[ k + 3 = 1 + 2k \;\Longrightarrow\; 3 - 1 = 2k - k \;\Longrightarrow\; k = 2. \]

Answer: \(\boxed{k = 2}\).

Check: With \(k = 2\): left piece at 1 gives \(2 + 3 = 5\); right piece gives \(1 + 4 = 5\) ✓. The two pieces meet with no jump.
