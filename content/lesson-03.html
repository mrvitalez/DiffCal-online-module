2.1Derivatives and Rates of Change

Chapter 1 gave you limits. Stewart §2.1 now uses one special limit to answer two old questions at once: how steep is a curve at a point? and how fast is something moving at an instant? Both answers turn out to be the same limit, called the derivative.

The tangent problem. Take a curve \(y = f(x)\) and a point \(P(a, f(a))\) on it. Pick a second point \(Q(x, f(x))\) nearby. The line through \(P\) and \(Q\) is a secant line, with slope \[m_{PQ} = \frac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a}.\] Now slide \(Q\) along the curve toward \(P\). The secant lines swing around and settle on one line through \(P\): the tangent line. Its slope is the limit of the secant slopes.

Definition The tangent line to \(y = f(x)\) at \(P(a, f(a))\) is the line through \(P\) with slope \[m = \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h},\] provided this limit exists.

The second form comes from writing \(x = a + h\), so \(h\) is the horizontal distance from \(P\) to \(Q\). It is usually easier to compute.

A small example. For \(f(x) = x^2 - 3x\) at \(a = 2\) (where \(f(2) = -2\)): \[\frac{f(2 + h) - f(2)}{h} = \frac{(4 + 4h + h^2 - 6 - 3h) - (-2)}{h} = \frac{h + h^2}{h} = 1 + h.\] As \(h \to 0\) this tends to 1, so the tangent at \((2, -2)\) has slope 1 and equation \(y + 2 = 1(x - 2)\), that is, \(y = x - 4\). Notice the pattern: plugging in \(h = 0\) at the start gives \(0/0\), so you must simplify and cancel the \(h\) before taking the limit.

The velocity problem. If \(s = f(t)\) is the position of an object at time \(t\), its average velocity from \(t = a\) to \(t = a + h\) is \(\dfrac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}\): change in position over change in time. Shrinking the time interval gives the instantaneous velocity \(v(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}\). For a stone falling with \(s = 4.9t^2\) metres, the same algebra gives \(v(a) = 9.8a\), so after 3 seconds it falls at \(29.4\) m/s.

The derivative at a point. Because this limit keeps appearing, it gets its own name and symbol: \[f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}.\] Geometrically, \(f'(a)\) is the slope of the tangent line at \(x = a\), so that tangent line is \(y - f(a) = f'(a)(x - a)\).

Rates of change. For any quantity \(y = f(x)\), the difference quotient \(\Delta y/\Delta x\) is the average rate of change over an interval, and \(f'(a)\) is the instantaneous rate of change of \(y\) with respect to \(x\) at \(x = a\). Its units are (units of \(y\)) per (unit of \(x\)): metres per second, pesos per item, degrees per hour. A large \(|f'(a)|\) means \(y\) changes quickly near \(a\); a small one means it changes slowly.

Common mistake \(f(a + h)\) is not \(f(a) + h\). Replace every \(x\) in the formula by \((a + h)\), with brackets, and expand carefully.
Key equations
\[ m = \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a} \]
\[ f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h} \]
\[ y - f(a) = f'(a)(x - a) \]

2.2The Derivative as a Function

In §2.1 you found \(f'(a)\) one number at a time. Stewart §2.2 lets the point vary: replace \(a\) by \(x\) and you get a whole new function.

Definition The derivative of \(f\) is the function \[f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h}.\] Its domain is the set of \(x\) where this limit exists.

For \(f(x) = x^2 + 5x\): \[\frac{(x + h)^2 + 5(x + h) - x^2 - 5x}{h} = \frac{2xh + h^2 + 5h}{h} = 2x + h + 5 \;\to\; 2x + 5.\] So \(f'(x) = 2x + 5\), a formula that gives the slope at every point. For instance \(f'(1) = 7\) and \(f'(-2.5) = 0\), so the parabola has a horizontal tangent at \(x = -2.5\). The domain of \(f'\) can be smaller than that of \(f\): Example 2 shows a square root whose derivative fails to exist at an endpoint.

Notation. If \(y = f(x)\), all of these mean the same thing: \[f'(x) = y' = \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{df}{dx} = \frac{d}{dx}f(x) = Df(x).\] The Leibniz form \(dy/dx\) reminds you of \(\Delta y/\Delta x\). To show a value at a point, write \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\Big|_{x = a}\), which means \(f'(a)\).

Differentiability. \(f\) is differentiable at \(a\) if \(f'(a)\) exists, and differentiable on an open interval if it is differentiable at every point of it.

Theorem If \(f\) is differentiable at \(a\), then \(f\) is continuous at \(a\).

The reason: \(f(a + h) - f(a) = \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}\cdot h \to f'(a)\cdot 0 = 0\). The converse is false: \(f(x) = |x|\) is continuous at 0, but its secant slopes are \(+1\) from the right and \(-1\) from the left, so \(f'(0)\) does not exist.

How a function can fail to be differentiable at \(a\):

  • a corner (or kink), where the left and right slopes differ, like \(|x|\) at 0;
  • a discontinuity (hole, jump or infinite break), by the theorem above;
  • a vertical tangent, where the curve is continuous but the slopes grow without bound, like \(y = \sqrt[3]{x}\) at 0.

Higher derivatives. \(f'\) is a function, so you can differentiate it again. The second derivative is \(f'' = (f')'\), written \(\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2}\); then come \(f'''\) and \(f^{(4)}, f^{(5)}, \ldots\). If \(s(t)\) is position, then \(v = s'\) is velocity and \(a = v' = s''\) is acceleration, the rate at which velocity changes.

Graphing \(f'\) from \(f\). The height of \(f'\) at \(x\) is the slope of \(f\) at \(x\). So \(f' \gt 0\) where \(f\) rises, \(f' \lt 0\) where \(f\) falls, and \(f' = 0\) at horizontal tangents (the peaks and valleys of \(f\)). Steep parts of \(f\) give large values of \(|f'|\).

Common mistake The zeros of \(f'\) are at the peaks and valleys of \(f\), not where \(f\) crosses the \(x\)-axis. Read slopes from the graph of \(f\), not heights.
Key equations
\[ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} \]
\[ f'(x) = y' = \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{d}{dx}f(x) = Df(x) \]
\[ a(t) = v'(t) = s''(t) \]

★Key takeaways

  • The derivative f′(a) is the limit of the difference quotient [f(a + h) − f(a)]/h as h → 0.
  • Geometrically f′(a) is the slope of the tangent line, and the tangent line is y − f(a) = f′(a)(x − a).
  • Physically f′(a) is an instantaneous rate of change, such as velocity, measured in units of y per unit of x.
  • Letting a vary gives the derivative function f′(x); differentiating again gives f″, such as acceleration.
  • Differentiable implies continuous, but not the reverse: corners, breaks and vertical tangents all stop f′ from existing.
  • The graph of f′ is above the axis where f rises, below where f falls, and zero at the peaks and valleys of f.

Example 3.1 Tangent line from the definition

Let \(f(x) = 2x^2 - 5x + 1\). Use the definition of the derivative to find \(f'(3)\), then find the equation of the tangent line to \(y = f(x)\) at \(x = 3\).

(Similar to Stewart §2.1, tangent lines)
Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Use \(f'(3) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(3 + h) - f(3)}{h}\). Expand \(f(3 + h)\), cancel, divide by \(h\), and only then let \(h \to 0\). The tangent line passes through \((3, f(3))\) with slope \(f'(3)\).

Solution:

First, \(f(3) = 2(9) - 15 + 1 = 4\). Next, \[f(3 + h) = 2(3 + h)^2 - 5(3 + h) + 1 = 2(9 + 6h + h^2) - 15 - 5h + 1 = 4 + 7h + 2h^2.\] So \[\frac{f(3 + h) - f(3)}{h} = \frac{4 + 7h + 2h^2 - 4}{h} = \frac{h(7 + 2h)}{h} = 7 + 2h \quad (h \ne 0).\] Therefore \(f'(3) = \lim_{h \to 0}(7 + 2h) = 7\).

Tangent line: \(y - 4 = 7(x - 3)\), so \(y = 7x - 21 + 4 = 7x - 17\).

Check:

The line must touch the curve at \(x = 3\): \(7(3) - 17 = 4 = f(3)\). Also, a secant from \(x = 3\) to \(x = 3.01\) has slope \(7 + 2(0.01) = 7.02\), very close to 7.

Example 3.2 A derivative function with a square root

Let \(f(x) = \sqrt{4x + 1}\). Use the definition to find \(f'(x)\). State the domains of \(f\) and \(f'\).

(Similar to Stewart §2.2, derivative from the definition)
Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

The difference quotient has a difference of square roots in the numerator. Multiply top and bottom by the conjugate \(\sqrt{4x + 4h + 1} + \sqrt{4x + 1}\) so the roots disappear from the numerator and the \(h\) can cancel.

Solution:

\[\frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} = \frac{\sqrt{4x + 4h + 1} - \sqrt{4x + 1}}{h}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{4x + 4h + 1} + \sqrt{4x + 1}}{\sqrt{4x + 4h + 1} + \sqrt{4x + 1}}.\] The numerator becomes \((4x + 4h + 1) - (4x + 1) = 4h\), so \[\frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} = \frac{4h}{h\left(\sqrt{4x + 4h + 1} + \sqrt{4x + 1}\right)} = \frac{4}{\sqrt{4x + 4h + 1} + \sqrt{4x + 1}}.\] Now let \(h \to 0\): \[f'(x) = \frac{4}{\sqrt{4x + 1} + \sqrt{4x + 1}} = \frac{4}{2\sqrt{4x + 1}} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{4x + 1}}.\]

Domains: \(f\) needs \(4x + 1 \ge 0\), so \(x \ge -\tfrac14\). \(f'\) also needs a nonzero denominator, so \(x \gt -\tfrac14\). At the endpoint \(x = -\tfrac14\) the graph has a vertical tangent.

Check:

At \(x = 2\): \(f'(2) = 2/\sqrt9 = 2/3\). Numerically, \(\frac{\sqrt{9.04} - 3}{0.01} \approx 0.666\). It agrees.

Example 3.3 Velocity and acceleration of a particle

A particle moves along a line with position \(s(t) = t^3 - 6t^2\) metres after \(t\) seconds (\(t \ge 0\)). (a) Use the definition to find the velocity \(v(t)\). (b) Find the acceleration \(a(t)\). (c) When is the particle at rest for \(t \gt 0\), and what are its position and acceleration then?

(Similar to Stewart §2.2, higher derivatives)
Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Velocity is \(s'(t)\), found from the limit definition. Acceleration is the derivative of velocity, \(a = v' = s''\), so apply the definition a second time. “at rest” means \(v(t) = 0\).

Solution:

(a) Expand \((t + h)^3 = t^3 + 3t^2h + 3th^2 + h^3\) and \((t + h)^2 = t^2 + 2th + h^2\): \[s(t + h) - s(t) = (3t^2h + 3th^2 + h^3) - 6(2th + h^2) = h\left(3t^2 + 3th + h^2 - 12t - 6h\right).\] Divide by \(h\) and let \(h \to 0\): \(v(t) = 3t^2 - 12t\) m/s.

(b) \(\dfrac{v(t + h) - v(t)}{h} = \dfrac{3(2th + h^2) - 12h}{h} = 6t + 3h - 12 \to 6t - 12\). So \(a(t) = 6t - 12\) m/s².

(c) \(v(t) = 3t(t - 4) = 0\) gives \(t = 0\) or \(t = 4\). For \(t \gt 0\), the particle is at rest at \(t = 4\) s. Then \(s(4) = 64 - 96 = -32\) m and \(a(4) = 24 - 12 = 12\) m/s².

Check:

For \(0 \lt t \lt 4\), \(v \lt 0\) (moving backward); for \(t \gt 4\), \(v \gt 0\). The positive acceleration at \(t = 4\) is what turns the particle around. Velocity zero does not mean acceleration zero.
