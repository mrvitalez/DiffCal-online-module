2.1Derivatives and Rates of Change

Let's recall our discussion on tangents from Module 2. We asked two questions that seemed impossible to answer: "What is the slope of the curve at this exact point?" and "What is the speed exactly at this instant?" In this section we answer both, and we will see that they are really the same question. The answer is the derivative.

Tangents. Consider a curve \(C\) with equation \(y = f(x)\), and let \(P(a, f(a))\) be a point on \(C\). We take another arbitrary point \(Q(x, f(x))\) on the curve, where \(x \ne a\). Again, we know that the slope of a line is easy, it is just rise over run, so we can compute the slope of the secant line \(PQ\):

\[ m_{PQ} = \frac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a} \]

Then we let \(Q\) approach \(P\) along the curve \(C\) by letting \(x\) approach \(a\). If \(m_{PQ}\) approaches a number \(m\), then we define the tangent \(t\) to be the line through \(P\) with slope \(m\).

Definition 1 (tangent line) The tangent line to the curve \(y = f(x)\) at the point \(P(a, f(a))\) is the line through \(P\) with slope \[ m = \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a} \] provided that this limit exists.

There is another expression for the slope of the tangent line that is sometimes easier to use. If we let \(h = x - a\), then \(x = a + h\), and the slope of the secant line \(PQ\) becomes \(\dfrac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}\). Notice that as \(x\) approaches \(a\), \(h\) approaches 0, and so the slope of the tangent line in Definition 1 becomes

\[ m = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h} \]

Velocities. In general, suppose an object moves along a straight line according to an equation of motion \(s = f(t)\), where \(s\) is the displacement of the object from the origin at time \(t\). The function \(f\) that describes the motion is called the position function of the object. In the time interval from \(t = a\) to \(t = a + h\), the change in position is \(f(a + h) - f(a)\), so the average velocity over that interval is

\[ \text{average velocity} = \frac{\text{displacement}}{\text{time}} = \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h} \]

This is exactly the slope of a secant line on the graph of \(f\). Now, suppose we compute the average velocities over shorter and shorter time intervals \([a, a + h]\). In other words, we let \(h\) approach 0. Then we define the velocity (or instantaneous velocity) \(v(a)\) at time \(t = a\) to be the limit of these average velocities:

Definition 2 (instantaneous velocity) \[ v(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h} \] This means that the velocity at \(t = a\) is equal to the slope of the tangent line at \(P\).

Derivatives. As we have seen, the limit of the form \(\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}\) arises whenever we calculate a rate of change in any of the sciences or engineering, such as the velocity of a falling body, a rate of reaction in chemistry, or marginal cost in economics. Since this type of limit occurs so widely, it is given a special name and notation.

Definition 3 (derivative at a number) The derivative of a function \(f\) at a number \(a\), denoted by \(f'(a)\), is \[ f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h} \] if this limit exists. If we write \(x = a + h\), then \(h = x - a\), and \(h \to 0\) if and only if \(x \to a\). So an equivalent way of stating the definition is \[ f'(a) = \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a}. \]

Putting everything together: the tangent line to \(y = f(x)\) at \((a, f(a))\) is the line through \((a, f(a))\) whose slope is \(f'(a)\). Using the point-slope form, its equation is \(y - f(a) = f'(a)(x - a)\).

Rates of change. Suppose \(y\) is a quantity that depends on another quantity \(x\), so \(y = f(x)\). If \(x\) changes from \(x_1\) to \(x_2\), the change in \(x\) is \(\Delta x = x_2 - x_1\) and the change in \(y\) is \(\Delta y = f(x_2) - f(x_1)\). The ratio \(\Delta y / \Delta x\) is the average rate of change of \(y\) with respect to \(x\). Letting \(\Delta x \to 0\) gives the instantaneous rate of change, which is the derivative \(f'(x_1)\). Take note that the units of a derivative are always "units of \(y\) per unit of \(x\)": metres per second for a velocity, pesos per item for marginal cost, degrees Celsius per kilometre for a temperature gradient.

Common mistake Substituting \(h = 0\) right away. Every difference quotient becomes \(\frac{0}{0}\) at \(h = 0\); that tells us nothing. We must first simplify (expand, combine fractions, or multiply by the conjugate) until the \(h\) in the denominator cancels, and only then let \(h \to 0\).
Key equations
\[ f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h} = \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a} \]
\[ y - f(a) = f'(a)(x - a) \quad \text{(tangent line)} \]
\[ v(a) = f'(a) \quad \text{(velocity = derivative of position)} \]

2.2The Derivative as a Function

In Section 2.1 we found the derivative of a function at one fixed number \(a\). But what if we want the slope at every point of the curve at once? If we replace the number \(a\) by the variable \(x\), we get a new function.

Definition (derivative as a function) Given a function \(f\), the derivative of \(f\) is the function \(f'\) defined by \[ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h}. \] The domain of \(f'\) is the set of all \(x\) for which this limit exists; it may be smaller than the domain of \(f\).

We can think of \(f'\) as a "slope machine": we put in \(x\), and it gives back the slope of the tangent line to the graph of \(f\) at \((x, f(x))\). For example, in Example 3.3 below we find that \(f(x) = x^2 - 8x + 9\) has \(f'(a) = 2a - 8\). Writing \(x\) for \(a\), the derivative function is \(f'(x) = 2x - 8\). Now we can get every slope we like without taking a new limit: \(f'(3) = -2\), \(f'(4) = 0\) (a horizontal tangent at the vertex), \(f'(5) = 2\).

Reading \(f'\) from the graph of \(f\). Where the graph of \(f\) rises, the tangent lines have positive slope, so \(f'(x) \gt 0\). Where it falls, \(f'(x) \lt 0\). At a smooth peak or valley the tangent is horizontal, so \(f'(x) = 0\). Notice that this lets us sketch \(f'\) from a picture of \(f\), even without a formula.

Other notations. If we use \(y = f(x)\), the following all mean the same thing:

\[ f'(x) = y' = \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{df}{dx} = \frac{d}{dx}f(x) = Df(x) = D_x f(x) \]

The symbol \(\frac{dy}{dx}\), introduced by Leibniz, should not be read as a fraction for now; it reminds us that the derivative is the limit of \(\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}\). To write the value at a number we use \(\left.\frac{dy}{dx}\right|_{x = a}\), which means \(f'(a)\).

When does a derivative fail to exist? A function \(f\) is differentiable at \(a\) if \(f'(a)\) exists, and differentiable on an open interval if it is differentiable at every number in the interval. Take note of this important theorem:

Theorem If \(f\) is differentiable at \(a\), then \(f\) is continuous at \(a\).

The converse is false: a function can be continuous and still not differentiable. There are three ways a derivative can fail to exist at \(a\):

  • A corner or kink: the left and right slopes differ, like \(y = |x|\) at 0 (slopes \(-1\) and \(1\)).
  • A discontinuity: by the theorem, if \(f\) is not continuous at \(a\), it cannot be differentiable there.
  • A vertical tangent: the curve is continuous but becomes vertical, so the slope goes to \(\pm\infty\), like \(y = \sqrt[3]{x}\) at 0.

Higher derivatives. If \(f\) is differentiable, then \(f'\) is also a function, so it may have a derivative of its own, \((f')' = f''\), called the second derivative. In Leibniz notation, \(f''(x) = \frac{d^2y}{dx^2}\). In physics this is very natural: if \(s(t)\) is the position of an object, then \(v(t) = s'(t)\) is its velocity and \(a(t) = v'(t) = s''(t)\) is its acceleration. For the falling ball in our examples, \(s(t) = 4.9t^2\) gives \(v(t) = 9.8t\) and \(a(t) = 9.8\) m/s², which is exactly the acceleration due to gravity.

Common mistake "Continuous means differentiable." It does not. \(y = |x|\) is continuous at 0 but has no tangent line there. What is true is the opposite direction: differentiable implies continuous.
Key equations
\[ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}, \qquad f''(x) = \frac{d}{dx}\big(f'(x)\big) = \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} \]
\[ v(t) = s'(t), \qquad a(t) = v'(t) = s''(t) \]

★Key takeaways

  • The slope of the tangent line at \(P(a, f(a))\) is the limit of the slopes of the secant lines: \(m = \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}\).
  • The same limit gives the instantaneous velocity when \(f\) is a position function. Tangent slope and velocity are the same idea.
  • This limit is the derivative \(f'(a)\); the tangent line is \(y - f(a) = f'(a)(x - a)\).
  • Never substitute \(h = 0\) first: simplify until the \(h\) in the denominator cancels.
  • Replacing \(a\) by \(x\) gives the derivative function \(f'(x)\), a slope machine for the whole curve.
  • Differentiable implies continuous, but not the other way around: corners, jumps and vertical tangents have no derivative.
  • The second derivative of position is acceleration: \(a(t) = v'(t) = s''(t)\).

Example 3.1 Tangent line to a hyperbola

Find an equation of the tangent line to the hyperbola \(y = \dfrac{3}{x}\) at the point \(P(3, 1)\).

(From the instructor's module)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: \(f(x) = \dfrac{3}{x}\), the point \((3, 1)\).   Find: the slope \(m\) and the equation of the tangent line.

Plan: Use the \(h\)-form of the slope, \(m = \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(3+h) - f(3)}{h}\). Combine the fractions so the \(h\) cancels, then use the point-slope form.

Step 1. Write the difference quotient at \(a = 3\). Since \(f(3) = 1\):

\[ m = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(3 + h) - f(3)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\frac{3}{3 + h} - 1}{h} \]

Step 2. Combine the two terms in the numerator over the common denominator \(3 + h\):

\[ \frac{3}{3 + h} - 1 = \frac{3 - (3 + h)}{3 + h} = \frac{-h}{3 + h} \]

Step 3. Divide by \(h\). Since \(h \ne 0\) while we take the limit, the \(h\) cancels:

\[ m = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{-h}{h(3 + h)} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{-1}{3 + h} \]

Step 4. Now it is safe to let \(h \to 0\):

\[ m = \frac{-1}{3 + 0} = -\frac{1}{3} \]

Step 5. Use the point-slope form \(y - y_1 = m(x - x_1)\) with \((x_1, y_1) = (3, 1)\), then clear the fraction:

\[ y - 1 = -\tfrac{1}{3}(x - 3) \;\Rightarrow\; 3y - 3 = -x + 3 \;\Rightarrow\; x + 3y - 6 = 0 \]

Answer: \(\boxed{x + 3y - 6 = 0}\), that is, \(y = -\tfrac13 x + 2\). That is our answer.

Check: The point \((3, 1)\) satisfies \(3 + 3(1) - 6 = 0\). A secant check: from \(x = 3\) to \(x = 3.01\), \(\frac{3/3.01 - 1}{0.01} \approx -0.332\), close to \(-\tfrac13\). The negative slope also matches the graph of \(3/x\), which falls for \(x \gt 0\).

Example 3.2 The velocity of a falling ball

Suppose that a ball is dropped from the upper floor of a building, 450 m above the ground. Find the velocity of the ball after 5 seconds.

(From the instructor's module)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: the ball is dropped (\(v_0 = 0\)), so the distance fallen is \(s(t) = \tfrac12 g t^2 = 4.9t^2\) metres.   Find: the instantaneous velocity at \(t = 5\) s.

Plan: Recall the kinematic equation for the distance fallen, then use Definition 2 with a general time \(t\) and substitute \(t = 5\) at the end.

Step 1. Recall the kinematic equation along the \(y\)-axis, with \(s_0 = 0\), \(v_0 = 0\) and \(g = 9.8\) m/s²:

\[ s(t) = s_0 + v_0 t + \tfrac12 g t^2 = 4.9t^2 \]

Step 2. Write the definition of velocity:

\[ v(t) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{s(t + h) - s(t)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{4.9(t + h)^2 - 4.9t^2}{h} \]

Step 3. Expand \((t + h)^2 = t^2 + 2th + h^2\) and cancel the \(4.9t^2\) terms:

\[ = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{4.9t^2 + 9.8th + 4.9h^2 - 4.9t^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{9.8th + 4.9h^2}{h} \]

Step 4. Factor \(h\) from the numerator and cancel it:

\[ = \lim_{h \to 0} (9.8t + 4.9h) = 9.8t \]

Step 5. Substitute \(t = 5\) s:

\[ v(5) = 9.8(5) = 49 \text{ m/s} \]

Answer: \(\boxed{v(5) = 49 \text{ m/s}}\), downward. That is our answer.

Check: In Module 2 we estimated this with a short interval: the average velocity from \(t = 5\) to \(t = 5.1\) s was \(\frac{4.9(5.1)^2 - 4.9(5)^2}{0.1} = 49.49\) m/s, and the averages approach 49 as the interval shrinks. Also \(v = gt = 9.8 \times 5 = 49\) m/s, as in physics. The ball has fallen \(4.9(25) = 122.5\) m, well short of 450 m, so it is still in the air.

Example 3.3 The derivative at a number a

Find the derivative of the function \(f(x) = x^2 - 8x + 9\) at the number \(a\).

(From the instructor's module)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: \(f(x) = x^2 - 8x + 9\).   Find: \(f'(a)\) for a general number \(a\).

Plan: Use Definition 3, \(f'(a) = \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}\). Expand, cancel, factor out \(h\).

Step 1. Find \(f(a + h)\) by replacing every \(x\) with \(a + h\), and expand:

\[ f(a + h) = (a + h)^2 - 8(a + h) + 9 = a^2 + 2ah + h^2 - 8a - 8h + 9 \]

Step 2. Subtract \(f(a) = a^2 - 8a + 9\). Watch the signs: the whole of \(f(a)\) is subtracted.

\[ f(a + h) - f(a) = 2ah + h^2 - 8h \]

Step 3. Divide by \(h\) and simplify:

\[ \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h} = \frac{h(2a + h - 8)}{h} = 2a + h - 8 \]

Step 4. Let \(h \to 0\):

\[ f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} (2a + h - 8) = 2a - 8 \]

Answer: \(\boxed{f'(a) = 2a - 8}\). That is our answer.

Check: At the vertex of the parabola, \(x = 4\), we get \(f'(4) = 0\), a horizontal tangent, as expected. At \(a = 5\): \(\frac{f(5.001) - f(5)}{0.001} = 2.001\), close to \(2(5) - 8 = 2\).

Example 3.4 Same derivative, the other definition, and a tangent line

Find the derivative of \(f(x) = x^2 - 8x + 9\) at the number \(a\) using the form \(f'(a) = \lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a}\), and find the tangent line to the parabola at the point \((3, -6)\).

(From the instructor's module)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: \(f(x) = x^2 - 8x + 9\), the point \((3, -6)\).   Find: f'(a) by the second definition, and the tangent line at \(x = 3\).

Plan: This time we do not expand \(f(a+h)\); instead we factor the numerator so that \(x - a\) cancels. Then substitute \(a = 3\) and use the point-slope form.

Step 1. Write the definition and subtract \(f(a)\) term by term:

\[ f'(a) = \lim_{x \to a} \frac{(x^2 - 8x + 9) - (a^2 - 8a + 9)}{x - a} = \lim_{x \to a} \frac{x^2 - a^2 - 8x + 8a}{x - a} \]

Step 2. Group and factor: \(x^2 - a^2 = (x - a)(x + a)\) (difference of two squares) and \(-8x + 8a = -8(x - a)\):

\[ = \lim_{x \to a} \frac{(x - a)(x + a) - 8(x - a)}{x - a} = \lim_{x \to a} \frac{(x - a)(x + a - 8)}{x - a} \]

Step 3. Cancel \(x - a\) (allowed because \(x \ne a\) inside the limit) and let \(x \to a\):

\[ f'(a) = \lim_{x \to a} (x + a - 8) = 2a - 8 \]

Step 4. The slope at \(a = 3\) is:

\[ m = f'(3) = 2(3) - 8 = -2 \]

Step 5. Point-slope form through \((3, -6)\):

\[ y - (-6) = -2(x - 3) \;\Rightarrow\; y + 6 = -2x + 6 \;\Rightarrow\; y = -2x \]

Answer: \(\boxed{f'(a) = 2a - 8}\) (the same as Example 3.3) and the tangent line is \(\boxed{y = -2x}\). That is our answer.

Check: The point is on the parabola: \(f(3) = 9 - 24 + 9 = -6\). ✓ The line passes through it: \(-2(3) = -6\). ✓ Both definitions gave the same \(f'(a)\), as they must.

Example 3.5 The derivative function and the second derivative

Let \(f(x) = \sqrt{x}\). (a) Use the definition to find \(f'(x)\) and state the domain of \(f'\). (b) Find the slope of the tangent at \(x = 4\).

Similar to Stewart §2.2, Example 2
Show solution (try it first)

Given: \(f(x) = \sqrt{x}\), domain \([0, \infty)\).   Find: \(f'(x)\), its domain, and \(f'(4)\).

Plan: The difference quotient has a root difference in the numerator, so we multiply by the conjugate, the same trick we used for limits in Module 2.

Step 1. Write the definition of the derivative function:

\[ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\sqrt{x + h} - \sqrt{x}}{h} \]

Step 2. Multiply the numerator and the denominator by the conjugate \(\sqrt{x + h} + \sqrt{x}\):

\[ = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(\sqrt{x + h} - \sqrt{x})(\sqrt{x + h} + \sqrt{x})}{h(\sqrt{x + h} + \sqrt{x})} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(x + h) - x}{h(\sqrt{x + h} + \sqrt{x})} \]

Step 3. Simplify the numerator to \(h\) and cancel:

\[ = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{1}{\sqrt{x + h} + \sqrt{x}} \]

Step 4. Let \(h \to 0\):

\[ f'(x) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}} \]

Step 5. Domain: we need \(\sqrt{x}\) defined and the denominator not zero, so \(x \gt 0\). At \(x = 0\) the tangent is vertical. Now evaluate at 4:

\[ f'(4) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{4}} = \frac14 \]

Answer: \(\boxed{f'(x) = \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}}\) with domain \((0, \infty)\), and \(f'(4) = \tfrac14\). That is our answer.

Check: \(\frac{\sqrt{4.01} - 2}{0.01} \approx 0.2498\), close to 0.25. ✓ Notice that the domain of \(f'\) is smaller than the domain of \(f\): the point \(x = 0\) is lost.
