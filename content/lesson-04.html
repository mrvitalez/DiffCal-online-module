2.3Differentiation Formulas

In Module 3 every derivative took a page of limit algebra. Do we need to do that every time? No. Stewart §2.3 gives us formulas. Each one is proved once from the definition, and then we can use it forever. Let's go through them one at a time, each with a sample.

RuleGeneral formSample
Derivative of a constant function\(\dfrac{d}{dx}(c) = 0\)\(\dfrac{d}{dx}(2) = 0\)
Power Rule\(\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^n) = nx^{n-1}\)\(\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^3) = 3x^2\)
Power Rule (negative power)\(\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^{-n}) = -nx^{-n-1}\)\(\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^{-3}) = -3x^{-4}\)
Constant Multiple Rule\(\dfrac{d}{dx}[cf(x)] = c\dfrac{d}{dx}f(x)\)\(\dfrac{d}{dx}(3x^3) = 3(3x^2) = 9x^2\)
Sum Rule\(\dfrac{d}{dx}[f(x) + g(x)] = \dfrac{d}{dx}f(x) + \dfrac{d}{dx}g(x)\)\(\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^5 + 3x^4) = 5x^4 + 12x^3\)
Difference Rule\(\dfrac{d}{dx}[f(x) - g(x)] = \dfrac{d}{dx}f(x) - \dfrac{d}{dx}g(x)\)\(\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^4 - 2x^3) = 4x^3 - 6x^2\)

Why is the derivative of a constant 0? Because the graph of \(y = c\) is a horizontal line, and a horizontal line has slope 0. Take note that the Power Rule works for every real number \(n\), not only positive whole numbers. So we rewrite roots and reciprocals as powers first: \(\sqrt{x} = x^{1/2}\) has derivative \(\tfrac12 x^{-1/2} = \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt x}\). Compare that with the conjugate trick we needed in Module 3.

With these rules we can differentiate any polynomial term by term: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}(2x^5 - 7x^2 + 4x - 9) = 10x^4 - 14x + 4\).

Products and quotients. What about a product like \((6x^3)(2x^2)\)? Is the derivative just the product of the derivatives? No. Do you know why? Try \(f = g = x\): \((x\cdot x)' = (x^2)' = 2x\), but \(f'g' = 1\cdot 1 = 1\). We need two more rules.

Product and Quotient Rules \[\frac{d}{dx}[f(x)g(x)] = g(x)\frac{d}{dx}f(x) + f(x)\frac{d}{dx}g(x)\] \[\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\right] = \frac{g(x)\dfrac{d}{dx}f(x) - f(x)\dfrac{d}{dx}g(x)}{[g(x)]^2}\]

In words, for the quotient: "low d-high minus high d-low, over low squared." The order in the numerator matters because of the minus sign. Example 1 works through a product and a quotient step by step and checks both by simplifying first.

One more sample with the Product Rule: \[\frac{d}{dx}\big[(x^2 + 1)(3x - 4)\big] = (3x - 4)(2x) + (x^2 + 1)(3) = 9x^2 - 8x + 3.\] We can confirm this by expanding first: \((x^2 + 1)(3x - 4) = 3x^3 - 4x^2 + 3x - 4\), whose derivative is again \(9x^2 - 8x + 3\). Notice also that we should not use the Quotient Rule when a simple rewrite works: \(\dfrac{3x^2 + \sqrt{x}}{x} = 3x + x^{-1/2}\) is quicker with the Power Rule.

Why two terms in the Product Rule? Picture a rectangle with sides \(f(x)\) and \(g(x)\), so its area is \(fg\). When \(x\) grows a little, both sides grow. The area gains a strip of about \(f\,\Delta g\) along one side and a strip of about \(g\,\Delta f\) along the other, plus a tiny corner \(\Delta f\,\Delta g\) that becomes negligible. One change on each side gives one term each.

Tangent and normal lines. Recall that at a point \((a, f(a))\) the tangent line has slope \(m = f'(a)\). The normal line is the line through the same point that is perpendicular to the tangent line, so its slope is \(-1/m\) (when \(m \ne 0\)). If \(m = 0\), the tangent is horizontal and the normal is the vertical line \(x = a\). Example 2 finds both lines (Figure 4.1).

Common mistake The derivative of a product is not the product of the derivatives, and the derivative of a quotient is not the quotient of the derivatives. Also, in the Quotient Rule, multiply the bottom by itself: \(g(x)^2\), not \(g(x^2)\).
Key equations
\[ \frac{d}{dx}(c) = 0, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}(x^n) = nx^{n-1} \]
\[ \frac{d}{dx}[cf(x)] = c\frac{d}{dx}f(x), \qquad \frac{d}{dx}[f(x) \pm g(x)] = \frac{d}{dx}f(x) \pm \frac{d}{dx}g(x) \]
\[ \frac{d}{dx}[f(x)g(x)] = g(x)\frac{d}{dx}f(x) + f(x)\frac{d}{dx}g(x) \]
\[ \frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\right] = \frac{g(x)\frac{d}{dx}f(x) - f(x)\frac{d}{dx}g(x)}{[g(x)]^2} \]

2.4Derivatives of Trigonometric Functions

Now let's differentiate the trigonometric functions. Before we can differentiate \(\sin x\), we need two limits about angles. Take note: in calculus, angles are always measured in radians. The formulas below are false in degrees.

Two key limits \[\lim_{\theta \to 0} \frac{\sin\theta}{\theta} = 1, \qquad \lim_{\theta \to 0} \frac{\cos\theta - 1}{\theta} = 0.\]

Why is the first one true? On the unit circle, an angle \(\theta\) cuts off an arc of length \(\theta\) and a vertical segment of height \(\sin\theta\). For small angles the arc and the segment are almost the same length, so their ratio approaches 1. Stewart §2.4 makes this precise with the Squeeze Theorem, and Figure 4.2 shows the graph of \(\sin\theta/\theta\) heading to 1. The second limit follows from the first by multiplying by \(\cos\theta + 1\) over itself.

We can use these limits for variations too. Since \(2x \to 0\) as \(x \to 0\), \[\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin 2x}{7x} = \frac{2}{7}\lim_{x \to 0}\frac{\sin 2x}{2x} = \frac{2}{7}\cdot 1 = \frac{2}{7}.\] Notice the trick: we make the denominator match the angle inside the sine.

The derivative of sine. Using \(\sin(x + h) = \sin x\cos h + \cos x\sin h\), \[\frac{\sin(x + h) - \sin x}{h} = \sin x\cdot\frac{\cos h - 1}{h} + \cos x\cdot\frac{\sin h}{h} \;\to\; \sin x\cdot 0 + \cos x\cdot 1.\] So \(\dfrac{d}{dx}(\sin x) = \cos x\). A similar argument gives \(\dfrac{d}{dx}(\cos x) = -\sin x\). The other four come from the Quotient Rule. For example, \[\frac{d}{dx}(\tan x) = \frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}\right) = \frac{\cos x\cos x - \sin x(-\sin x)}{\cos^2 x} = \frac{1}{\cos^2 x} = \sec^2 x.\]

DerivativeSample
\(\dfrac{d}{dx}(\sin x) = \cos x\)\(\dfrac{d}{dx}(x\cos x) = \cos x - x\sin x\) (Product Rule)
\(\dfrac{d}{dx}(\cos x) = -\sin x\)\(\dfrac{d}{d\theta}\left(\dfrac{\cos\theta}{1 - \sin\theta}\right) = \dfrac{1}{1 - \sin\theta}\) (Example 3)
\(\dfrac{d}{dx}(\tan x) = \sec^2 x\)\(\dfrac{d}{dx}(2\tan x) = 2\sec^2 x\)
\(\dfrac{d}{dx}(\csc x) = -\csc x\cot x\)\(\dfrac{d}{dx}(2\sec x - \csc x) = 2\sec x\tan x + \csc x\cot x\)
\(\dfrac{d}{dx}(\sec x) = \sec x\tan x\)\(\dfrac{d}{dx}(\sec x\tan x) = \sec^3 x + \sec x\tan^2 x\) (Example 4)
\(\dfrac{d}{dx}(\cot x) = -\csc^2 x\)\(\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^2\cot x) = 2x\cot x - x^2\csc^2 x\) (Example 4)

Here is a pattern that helps us remember the signs: the derivatives of the "co" functions (cosine, cosecant, cotangent) all have a minus sign. In the sample \(2\sec x - \csc x\), notice how the two minus signs meet: \(-(-\csc x\cot x) = +\csc x\cot x\).

Repeating pattern. If we differentiate \(\sin x\) again and again, we get \(\cos x,\ -\sin x,\ -\cos x,\ \sin x\), and then the cycle repeats every four steps. This is useful for motion that goes back and forth. If a mass on a spring has position \(s(t) = 4\sin t\) cm, its velocity is \(v = 4\cos t\) and its acceleration is \(a = -4\sin t = -s\). The acceleration always points back toward the rest position, which is why the mass keeps oscillating. Figure 4.3 shows how the graph of \(\cos x\) records the slopes of \(\sin x\).

Common mistake \(\dfrac{\sin 2x}{x}\) does not approach 1, and we cannot cancel the \(x\) out of \(\sin 2x\). The limit \(\sin\theta/\theta \to 1\) needs the same quantity in the sine and in the denominator.
Key equations
\[ \lim_{\theta \to 0} \frac{\sin\theta}{\theta} = 1, \qquad \lim_{\theta \to 0} \frac{\cos\theta - 1}{\theta} = 0 \]
\[ \frac{d}{dx}(\sin x) = \cos x, \quad \frac{d}{dx}(\cos x) = -\sin x, \quad \frac{d}{dx}(\tan x) = \sec^2 x \]
\[ \frac{d}{dx}(\csc x) = -\csc x\cot x, \quad \frac{d}{dx}(\sec x) = \sec x\tan x, \quad \frac{d}{dx}(\cot x) = -\csc^2 x \]

★Key takeaways

  • The derivative of a constant is 0, and the Power Rule d/dx(xⁿ) = nxⁿ⁻¹ works for every real n, so rewrite roots and reciprocals as powers first.
  • Constants come out in front, and sums and differences are differentiated term by term.
  • The Product Rule is (fg)′ = g f′ + f g′ and the Quotient Rule is (f/g)′ = (g f′ − f g′)/g²; neither is the product or quotient of derivatives.
  • The normal line at a point is perpendicular to the tangent line, with slope −1/f′(a).
  • With θ in radians, (sin θ)/θ → 1 and (cos θ − 1)/θ → 0 as θ → 0; these give (sin x)′ = cos x and (cos x)′ = −sin x.
  • The other trig derivatives come from the Quotient Rule; the "co" functions all have a minus sign.

Example 4.1 Product and Quotient Rules, checked by simplifying

(a) Use the Product Rule to find \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\left[(6x^3)(2x^2)\right]\). (b) Use the Quotient Rule to find \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\left[\dfrac{x^2}{2x^3}\right]\). Check each answer by simplifying the function first.

(Instr. Vitalez, Module 2, Differentiation Formulas samples (compare Stewart §2.3))
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • (a) \(f(x) = 6x^3\), \(g(x) = 2x^2\); find \((fg)'\).
  • (b) top \(f(x) = x^2\), bottom \(g(x) = 2x^3\); find \((f/g)'\).
Step 1: Find the derivatives of the pieces

Use the Constant Multiple and Power Rules on each piece.

\[\frac{d}{dx}(6x^3) = 18x^2, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}(2x^2) = 4x\]

\[\frac{d}{dx}(x^2) = 2x, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}(2x^3) = 6x^2\]

Step 2: Apply the Product Rule for (a)

\[\frac{d}{dx}\left[(6x^3)(2x^2)\right] = 2x^2\frac{d}{dx}(6x^3) + 6x^3\frac{d}{dx}(2x^2)\]

\[= 2x^2(18x^2) + 6x^3(4x)\]

\[= 36x^4 + 24x^4\]

\[= 60x^4\]

Step 3: Check (a) by multiplying first

\((6x^3)(2x^2) = 12x^5\), and \(\dfrac{d}{dx}(12x^5) = 60x^4\). It matches.

Step 4: Apply the Quotient Rule for (b)

Low d-high minus high d-low, over low squared.

\[\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{x^2}{2x^3}\right] = \frac{2x^3\frac{d}{dx}(x^2) - x^2\frac{d}{dx}(2x^3)}{(2x^3)^2}\]

\[= \frac{2x^3(2x) - x^2(6x^2)}{4x^6}\]

\[= \frac{4x^4 - 6x^4}{4x^6}\]

\[= \frac{-2x^4}{4x^6}\]

\[= -\frac{1}{2x^2}\]

Step 5: Check (b) by simplifying first

\(\dfrac{x^2}{2x^3} = \dfrac{1}{2x} = \tfrac12 x^{-1}\), so the derivative is \(\tfrac12(-1)x^{-2} = -\dfrac{1}{2x^2}\). It matches.

Answer

(a) \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\left[(6x^3)(2x^2)\right] = 60x^4\). (b) \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\left[\dfrac{x^2}{2x^3}\right] = -\dfrac{1}{2x^2}\).

Check

Take note of a common slip in (b): \(2x^3\cdot 2x\) is \(4x^4\), not \(2x^4\). Simplifying first is the quickest way to catch it.

Example 4.2 Tangent and normal lines with the Quotient Rule

Find the equations of the tangent line and the normal line to \(y = \dfrac{3x}{x^2 + 2}\) at the point \((1, 1)\).

(Similar to Stewart §2.3, normal lines)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • \(y = \dfrac{3x}{x^2 + 2}\), point \((1, 1)\).
  • Find: the tangent line (slope \(m = y'(1)\)) and the normal line (slope \(-1/m\)).
Step 1: Differentiate with the Quotient Rule

The top is \(3x\) (derivative 3) and the bottom is \(x^2 + 2\) (derivative \(2x\)).

\[y' = \frac{(x^2 + 2)(3) - 3x(2x)}{(x^2 + 2)^2}\]

\[= \frac{3x^2 + 6 - 6x^2}{(x^2 + 2)^2}\]

\[= \frac{6 - 3x^2}{(x^2 + 2)^2}\]

Step 2: Find the tangent slope at x = 1

\[m = \frac{6 - 3(1)^2}{(1^2 + 2)^2} = \frac{3}{9} = \frac{1}{3}\]

Step 3: Write the tangent line

\[y - 1 = \frac{1}{3}(x - 1)\]

\[y = \frac{1}{3}x - \frac{1}{3} + 1 = \frac{1}{3}x + \frac{2}{3}\]

Step 4: Write the normal line

The normal slope is \(-1/m = -1/(1/3) = -3\).

\[y - 1 = -3(x - 1)\]

\[y = -3x + 3 + 1 = -3x + 4\]

Answer

The tangent line is \(y = \tfrac13 x + \tfrac23\) and the normal line is \(y = -3x + 4\).

Check

Both lines give \(y = 1\) at \(x = 1\), and the product of the slopes is \(\tfrac13\cdot(-3) = -1\), so they are perpendicular. Figure 4.1 shows the picture.

Example 4.3 A trigonometric quotient that simplifies nicely

Find \(\dfrac{d}{d\theta}\left(\dfrac{\cos\theta}{1 - \sin\theta}\right)\).

(Instr. Vitalez, Module 2, Trigonometric Derivatives sample (compare Stewart §2.4))
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • Top: \(\cos\theta\), with derivative \(-\sin\theta\).
  • Bottom: \(1 - \sin\theta\), with derivative \(-\cos\theta\).
  • Find: the derivative with respect to \(\theta\).
Step 1: Apply the Quotient Rule

\[\frac{d}{d\theta}\left(\frac{\cos\theta}{1 - \sin\theta}\right) = \frac{(1 - \sin\theta)\frac{d}{d\theta}(\cos\theta) - (\cos\theta)\frac{d}{d\theta}(1 - \sin\theta)}{(1 - \sin\theta)^2}\]

Step 2: Substitute the derivatives

\[= \frac{(1 - \sin\theta)(-\sin\theta) - (\cos\theta)(-\cos\theta)}{(1 - \sin\theta)^2}\]

Step 3: Multiply out the numerator

\[= \frac{-\sin\theta + \sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta}{(1 - \sin\theta)^2}\]

Step 4: Use the Pythagorean identity

Recall \(\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1\).

\[= \frac{1 - \sin\theta}{(1 - \sin\theta)^2}\]

Step 5: Cancel the common factor

\[= \frac{1}{1 - \sin\theta}\]

Answer

\(\dfrac{d}{d\theta}\left(\dfrac{\cos\theta}{1 - \sin\theta}\right) = \dfrac{1}{1 - \sin\theta}\) (for \(\sin\theta \ne 1\)).

Check

At \(\theta = 0\) the answer gives slope 1. Numerically, \(\frac{\cos(0.001)}{1 - \sin(0.001)} \approx 1.0010\) while the function equals 1 at \(\theta = 0\), a rise of about 0.001 over a run of 0.001, so the slope is about 1.

Example 4.4 Product Rule with sec, tan and cot

Differentiate (a) \(y = \sec x\tan x\) and (b) \(y = x^2\cot x\).

(Instr. Vitalez, Module 2, Trigonometric Derivatives samples (compare Stewart §2.4))
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • (a) a product of \(\sec x\) and \(\tan x\).
  • (b) a product of \(x^2\) and \(\cot x\).
  • We need: \((\sec x)' = \sec x\tan x\), \((\tan x)' = \sec^2 x\), \((\cot x)' = -\csc^2 x\), \((x^2)' = 2x\).
Step 1: Apply the Product Rule to (a)

\[\frac{d}{dx}(\sec x\tan x) = \sec x\frac{d}{dx}(\tan x) + \tan x\frac{d}{dx}(\sec x)\]

\[= \sec x(\sec^2 x) + \tan x(\sec x\tan x)\]

\[= \sec^3 x + \sec x\tan^2 x\]

Step 2: Apply the Product Rule to (b)

\[\frac{d}{dx}(x^2\cot x) = x^2\frac{d}{dx}(\cot x) + \cot x\frac{d}{dx}(x^2)\]

\[= x^2(-\csc^2 x) + \cot x(2x)\]

\[= 2x\cot x - x^2\csc^2 x\]

Answer

(a) \(\dfrac{d}{dx}(\sec x\tan x) = \sec^3 x + \sec x\tan^2 x\). (b) \(\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^2\cot x) = 2x\cot x - x^2\csc^2 x\).

Check

In (a) we may also use \(\tan^2 x = \sec^2 x - 1\) to write the answer as \(2\sec^3 x - \sec x\). In (b) the minus sign comes from the derivative of the \"co\" function \(\cot x\).

Example 4.5 Horizontal tangents of a trigonometric quotient

Let \(f(x) = \dfrac{\sin x}{2 + \cos x}\). Find \(f'(x)\) and all \(x\) in \([0, 2\pi]\) where the graph of \(f\) has a horizontal tangent.

(Similar to Stewart §2.4, horizontal tangents)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • \(f(x) = \dfrac{\sin x}{2 + \cos x}\) on \([0, 2\pi]\).
  • Find: \(f'(x)\), then the \(x\)-values where \(f'(x) = 0\).
Step 1: Apply the Quotient Rule

The top \(\sin x\) has derivative \(\cos x\); the bottom \(2 + \cos x\) has derivative \(-\sin x\).

\[f'(x) = \frac{(2 + \cos x)(\cos x) - (\sin x)(-\sin x)}{(2 + \cos x)^2}\]

Step 2: Simplify the numerator

Multiply out, then use \(\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1\).

\[= \frac{2\cos x + \cos^2 x + \sin^2 x}{(2 + \cos x)^2}\]

\[= \frac{2\cos x + 1}{(2 + \cos x)^2}\]

Step 3: Set the derivative equal to zero

A fraction is zero when its numerator is zero and its denominator is not. The denominator is never zero because \(2 + \cos x \ge 1\).

\[2\cos x + 1 = 0\]

\[\cos x = -\frac{1}{2}\]

Step 4: Solve in [0, 2π]

Cosine is \(-\tfrac12\) in the second and third quadrants, with reference angle \(\pi/3\).

\[x = \pi - \frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{2\pi}{3}, \qquad x = \pi + \frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{4\pi}{3}\]

Step 5: Find the y-values

\[f\left(\tfrac{2\pi}{3}\right) = \frac{\sqrt3/2}{2 - \tfrac12} = \frac{\sqrt3/2}{3/2} = \frac{\sqrt3}{3}, \qquad f\left(\tfrac{4\pi}{3}\right) = \frac{-\sqrt3/2}{3/2} = -\frac{\sqrt3}{3}\]

Answer

\(f'(x) = \dfrac{2\cos x + 1}{(2 + \cos x)^2}\), and the horizontal tangents are at \(x = \dfrac{2\pi}{3}\) and \(x = \dfrac{4\pi}{3}\), at the points \(\left(\tfrac{2\pi}{3}, \tfrac{\sqrt3}{3}\right)\) and \(\left(\tfrac{4\pi}{3}, -\tfrac{\sqrt3}{3}\right)\).

Check

\(f'(0) = 3/9 = 1/3 \gt 0\) and \(f'(\pi) = -1/1 = -1 \lt 0\), so \(f'\) changes sign between them, consistent with a zero at \(2\pi/3\).
