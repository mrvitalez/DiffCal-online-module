2.3Differentiation Formulas

In Module 3 every derivative took a page of limit algebra. Stewart §2.3 replaces that work with a handful of rules, each proved once from the definition and then used forever. Learn them well: everything else in this course rests on them.

Basic rules For a constant \(c\) and differentiable \(f, g\):
  • Constant: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}(c) = 0\) (a horizontal line has slope 0).
  • Power Rule: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^n) = nx^{n-1}\) for every real number \(n\).
  • Constant Multiple: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[cf(x)] = c\,f'(x)\).
  • Sum and Difference: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[f(x) \pm g(x)] = f'(x) \pm g'(x)\).

Together these let you differentiate any polynomial term by term: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}(2x^5 - 7x^2 + 4x - 9) = 10x^4 - 14x + 4\). The Power Rule also works for negative and fractional powers, so rewrite roots and reciprocals as powers first: \(\dfrac{1}{x^3} = x^{-3}\) has derivative \(-3x^{-4}\), and \(\sqrt{x} = x^{1/2}\) has derivative \(\tfrac12 x^{-1/2} = \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt x}\). Compare that with the long limit you needed in Module 3.

Product and Quotient Rules \[\frac{d}{dx}[f g] = f\,g' + g\,f', \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{f}{g}\right] = \frac{g\,f' - f\,g'}{g^2}.\]

A memory aid for the quotient: “low d-high minus high d-low, over low squared.” The order in the numerator matters because of the minus sign.

For example, with the Product Rule, \[\frac{d}{dx}\big[(x^2 + 1)(3x - 4)\big] = (x^2 + 1)(3) + (3x - 4)(2x) = 9x^2 - 8x + 3,\] which you can confirm by expanding first. With the Quotient Rule, \[\frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{x}{x^2 + 4}\right] = \frac{(x^2 + 4)(1) - x(2x)}{(x^2 + 4)^2} = \frac{4 - x^2}{(x^2 + 4)^2}.\] Do not use the Quotient Rule when a simple rewrite works: \(\dfrac{3x^2 + \sqrt{x}}{x} = 3x + x^{-1/2}\) is quicker by the Power Rule.

Why two terms in the Product Rule? Picture a rectangle with sides \(f(x)\) and \(g(x)\), so its area is \(fg\). When \(x\) grows a little, both sides grow. The area gains a strip along one side, about \(f\,\Delta g\), and a strip along the other, about \(g\,\Delta f\), plus a tiny corner piece \(\Delta f\,\Delta g\) that becomes negligible. Dividing by \(\Delta x\) and letting \(\Delta x \to 0\) leaves exactly \(f g' + g f'\). One change on each side gives one term each.

Tangent and normal lines. At a point \((a, f(a))\), the tangent line has slope \(m = f'(a)\). The normal line is the line through the same point that is perpendicular to the tangent, so its slope is \(-1/m\) (when \(m \ne 0\)). If \(m = 0\), the tangent is horizontal and the normal is the vertical line \(x = a\).

Common mistake The derivative of a product is not the product of the derivatives: \((fg)' \ne f'g'\). Test it with \(f = g = x\): \((x^2)' = 2x\), but \(f'g' = 1\). The same goes for quotients.
Key equations
\[ \frac{d}{dx}(x^n) = nx^{n-1} \]
\[ (fg)' = f g' + g f' \]
\[ \left(\frac{f}{g}\right)' = \frac{g f' - f g'}{g^2} \]

2.4Derivatives of Trigonometric Functions

Before you can differentiate \(\sin x\), you need two limits about angles. In calculus, angles are always measured in radians; the formulas below are false in degrees.

Two key limits \[\lim_{\theta \to 0} \frac{\sin\theta}{\theta} = 1, \qquad \lim_{\theta \to 0} \frac{\cos\theta - 1}{\theta} = 0.\]

Why is the first one true? On the unit circle, an angle \(\theta\) cuts off an arc of length \(\theta\) and a vertical segment of height \(\sin\theta\). For small angles the arc and the segment are almost the same length, so their ratio approaches 1. Stewart §2.4 makes this precise with a Squeeze Theorem argument comparing areas, and Figure 4.2 shows the graph of \(\sin\theta/\theta\) heading to 1. The second limit follows from the first by multiplying by \(\cos\theta + 1\) over itself.

These limits handle variations too. Since \(2x \to 0\) as \(x \to 0\), \[\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin 2x}{7x} = \frac{2}{7}\lim_{x \to 0}\frac{\sin 2x}{2x} = \frac{2}{7}\cdot 1 = \frac{2}{7}.\] The trick is to make the denominator match the angle inside the sine.

The derivative of sine. Using \(\sin(x + h) = \sin x\cos h + \cos x\sin h\), \[\frac{\sin(x + h) - \sin x}{h} = \sin x\cdot\frac{\cos h - 1}{h} + \cos x\cdot\frac{\sin h}{h} \;\to\; \sin x\cdot 0 + \cos x\cdot 1.\] So \(\dfrac{d}{dx}(\sin x) = \cos x\). A similar argument gives \(\dfrac{d}{dx}(\cos x) = -\sin x\). The other four follow from the Quotient Rule, for example \[\frac{d}{dx}(\tan x) = \frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}\right) = \frac{\cos x\cos x - \sin x(-\sin x)}{\cos^2 x} = \frac{1}{\cos^2 x} = \sec^2 x.\]

A pattern helps you remember the signs: the derivatives of the “co” functions (cosine, cosecant, cotangent) all carry a minus sign.

Combine the table with the rules of §2.3. For instance \(\dfrac{d}{dx}(x\cos x) = \cos x - x\sin x\) by the Product Rule, and \(\dfrac{d}{dx}(3\tan x - 2\sec x) = 3\sec^2 x - 2\sec x\tan x\) by the Sum and Constant Multiple Rules.

Repeating pattern. Differentiating \(\sin x\) again and again gives \(\cos x,\ -\sin x,\ -\cos x,\ \sin x\), and then the cycle repeats every four steps. This is useful for motion that goes back and forth. If a mass on a spring has position \(s(t) = 4\sin t\) cm, its velocity is \(v = 4\cos t\) and its acceleration is \(a = -4\sin t = -s\). The acceleration always points back toward the rest position, which is exactly why the mass keeps oscillating. Figure 4.3 shows how the graph of \(\cos x\) records the slopes of \(\sin x\).

Common mistake \(\dfrac{\sin 2x}{x}\) does not tend to 1, and you cannot cancel the \(x\) out of \(\sin 2x\). The limit \(\sin\theta/\theta \to 1\) needs the same quantity in the sine and the denominator.
Key equations
\[ \lim_{\theta \to 0} \frac{\sin\theta}{\theta} = 1 \]
\[ \frac{d}{dx}(\sin x) = \cos x, \quad \frac{d}{dx}(\cos x) = -\sin x \]
\[ \frac{d}{dx}(\tan x) = \sec^2 x \]

★Key takeaways

  • Rewrite roots and reciprocals as powers, then use the Power Rule d/dx(xⁿ) = nxⁿ⁻¹ term by term.
  • The Product Rule is (fg)′ = fg′ + gf′ and the Quotient Rule is (f/g)′ = (gf′ − fg′)/g²; neither is the product or quotient of derivatives.
  • The normal line at a point is perpendicular to the tangent line, with slope −1/f′(a).
  • With θ in radians, (sin θ)/θ → 1 and (cos θ − 1)/θ → 0 as θ → 0; these give (sin x)′ = cos x and (cos x)′ = −sin x.
  • The other trig derivatives come from the Quotient Rule; the "co" functions all have a minus sign.

Example 4.1 Power Rule with roots and reciprocals

Differentiate \(y = 4x^5 - \dfrac{3}{x^2} + 6\sqrt{x}\).

(Similar to Stewart §2.3, Power Rule)
Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Rewrite every term as a constant times a power of \(x\), then use the Power, Constant Multiple and Sum rules term by term.

Solution:

Rewrite: \(y = 4x^5 - 3x^{-2} + 6x^{1/2}\). Differentiate each term: \[y' = 4(5x^4) - 3(-2x^{-3}) + 6\left(\tfrac12 x^{-1/2}\right) = 20x^4 + 6x^{-3} + 3x^{-1/2}.\] In root form: \(y' = 20x^4 + \dfrac{6}{x^3} + \dfrac{3}{\sqrt{x}}\).

Check:

Watch the signs: \(-3\) times \(-2\) is \(+6\). The power drops by one each time: \(-2 - 1 = -3\) and \(\tfrac12 - 1 = -\tfrac12\). A common slip is writing \(x^{-1}\) for the derivative of \(x^{-2}\).

Example 4.2 Tangent and normal lines with the Quotient Rule

Find the equations of the tangent line and the normal line to \(y = \dfrac{3x}{x^2 + 2}\) at the point \((1, 1)\).

(Similar to Stewart §2.3, normal lines)
Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Find \(y'\) with the Quotient Rule, evaluate at \(x = 1\) to get the tangent slope \(m\), then use slope \(-1/m\) for the normal line. Both lines pass through \((1, 1)\).

Solution:

With top \(3x\) and bottom \(x^2 + 2\): \[y' = \frac{(x^2 + 2)(3) - 3x(2x)}{(x^2 + 2)^2} = \frac{3x^2 + 6 - 6x^2}{(x^2 + 2)^2} = \frac{6 - 3x^2}{(x^2 + 2)^2}.\] At \(x = 1\): \(m = \dfrac{6 - 3}{9} = \dfrac13\).

Tangent: \(y - 1 = \tfrac13(x - 1)\), so \(y = \tfrac13 x + \tfrac23\).

Normal: slope \(-1/(1/3) = -3\), so \(y - 1 = -3(x - 1)\), that is, \(y = -3x + 4\).

Check:

Both lines give \(y = 1\) at \(x = 1\), and the product of the slopes is \(\tfrac13\cdot(-3) = -1\), so they are perpendicular. Figure 4.1 shows the picture.

Example 4.3 Horizontal tangents of a trigonometric quotient

Let \(f(x) = \dfrac{\sin x}{2 + \cos x}\). Find \(f'(x)\) and all \(x\) in \([0, 2\pi]\) where the graph of \(f\) has a horizontal tangent.

(Similar to Stewart §2.4, horizontal tangents)
Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Use the Quotient Rule with the trig derivatives, simplify with \(\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1\), then solve \(f'(x) = 0\). A fraction is zero exactly when its numerator is zero (and its denominator is not).

Solution:

\[f'(x) = \frac{(2 + \cos x)(\cos x) - (\sin x)(-\sin x)}{(2 + \cos x)^2} = \frac{2\cos x + \cos^2 x + \sin^2 x}{(2 + \cos x)^2} = \frac{2\cos x + 1}{(2 + \cos x)^2}.\] The denominator is never zero, because \(2 + \cos x \ge 1\). So \(f'(x) = 0\) when \(2\cos x + 1 = 0\), that is, \(\cos x = -\tfrac12\).

In \([0, 2\pi]\) this happens at \(x = \dfrac{2\pi}{3}\) and \(x = \dfrac{4\pi}{3}\). The points are \(\left(\tfrac{2\pi}{3}, \tfrac{\sqrt3}{3}\right)\) and \(\left(\tfrac{4\pi}{3}, -\tfrac{\sqrt3}{3}\right)\), since \(f\left(\tfrac{2\pi}{3}\right) = \dfrac{\sqrt3/2}{3/2} = \dfrac{\sqrt3}{3}\).

Check:

At \(x = 0\), \(f'(0) = 3/9 = 1/3 \gt 0\), and at \(x = \pi\), \(f'(\pi) = -1/1 = -1 \lt 0\). So \(f'\) changes sign between them, consistent with a zero at \(2\pi/3\).
