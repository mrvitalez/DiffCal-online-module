2.7Rates of Change in the Natural and Social Sciences

Recall from Module 3 that if \(y = f(x)\), then the derivative \(dy/dx\) is the instantaneous rate of change of \(y\) with respect to \(x\). We have been using this mostly as a slope. In this section, let's see how the very same derivative appears all over the sciences, each time with its own name. The mathematics does not change; only the units and the interpretation do.

Physics: velocity and acceleration. If \(s = f(t)\) is the position of a particle moving along a straight line, then

\[ v(t) = \frac{ds}{dt}, \qquad a(t) = \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{d^2s}{dt^2}. \]

The sign of \(v\) tells us the direction: \(v \gt 0\) means moving forward (in the positive direction), \(v \lt 0\) means moving backward, and \(v = 0\) means the particle is momentarily at rest. Take note that a particle at rest may be turning around, as in Figure 6.1. The speed is \(|v|\). The particle is speeding up when \(v\) and \(a\) have the same sign, and slowing down when they have opposite signs.

Because a particle can turn around, the total distance travelled is not always \(|s(\text{end}) - s(\text{start})|\). We must find the times when \(v = 0\), split the interval there, and add the distances of each piece.

Other sciences. The same idea appears everywhere:

  • Chemistry: if \([P]\) is the concentration of a product at time \(t\), the rate of reaction is \(d[P]/dt\), in mol/L per second.
  • Biology: if \(n = f(t)\) is the size of a population, \(dn/dt\) is its growth rate, in individuals per unit time.
  • Astronomy: if \(r(t)\) is the distance of a comet from the Sun, \(dr/dt\) is its radial velocity, which is negative while it approaches and positive while it recedes.
  • Economics: if \(C(x)\) is the cost of producing \(x\) units, \(C'(x)\) is the marginal cost. Since \(C'(x) \approx \frac{C(x + 1) - C(x)}{1}\), the marginal cost is approximately the cost of producing one more unit.
Common mistake Reading \(C'(500) = 14\) as "500 units cost 14 pesos." A derivative is a rate, not an amount: it says that, at a production level of 500 units, the cost is increasing by about 14 pesos per additional unit. Always state a derivative with "per" in its units.
Key equations
\[ v = \frac{ds}{dt}, \quad a = \frac{dv}{dt}, \quad \text{speed} = |v| \]
\[ C'(x) \approx C(x + 1) - C(x) \quad \text{(marginal cost)} \]

2.8Related Rates

Imagine we are pumping air into a balloon. Both the volume and the radius are increasing, and the two rates are connected: if we know how fast the volume grows, can we find how fast the radius grows? Yes. Problems like this, where we know one rate and want another, are called related rates problems.

The idea is simple: if two quantities are related by an equation, then their rates of change are related by the derivative of that equation with respect to time \(t\). Every quantity that changes is treated as a function of \(t\), so the Chain Rule (implicit differentiation, Module 5) gives the connection. For example, from \(V = \frac43\pi r^3\) we get \(\frac{dV}{dt} = 4\pi r^2\frac{dr}{dt}\).

Problem-solving strategy
  1. Read the problem carefully, and draw a diagram if possible.
  2. Introduce notation. Assign symbols to all quantities that are functions of time.
  3. Write down the given information and the required rate in terms of derivatives (for example, "the radius grows at 2 cm/s" means \(dr/dt = 2\)).
  4. Write an equation that relates the quantities. If necessary, use the geometry (similar triangles, the Pythagorean Theorem) to eliminate a variable.
  5. Differentiate both sides with respect to \(t\), using the Chain Rule.
  6. Only then substitute the given information at the instant of interest, and solve for the unknown rate.

Two classic situations are the conical tank (Figure 6.3), where similar triangles relate the radius and the depth of the water, and the sliding ladder (Figure 6.4), where the Pythagorean Theorem relates the positions of the foot and the top.

Common mistake Substituting the numbers too early. If we put \(h = 2\) into the equation before differentiating, \(h\) becomes a constant, its derivative becomes 0, and the rate we want disappears. Differentiate first (step 5), substitute last (step 6). Also watch the signs: a quantity that is decreasing has a negative rate.
Key equations
\[ \frac{d}{dt}\left(x^2 + y^2\right) = 2x\frac{dx}{dt} + 2y\frac{dy}{dt}, \qquad \frac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac43\pi r^3\right) = 4\pi r^2\frac{dr}{dt} \]

2.9Linear Approximations and Differentials

Before calculators, how did people compute \(\sqrt{16.5}\) or \(\sin 31^\circ\)? And today, how does an engineer estimate how much a measurement error affects a computed result? Both questions are answered by one idea: near a point, a curve looks like its tangent line. If we zoom in on a smooth curve, it becomes almost straight.

Definition (linearization) The linearization of \(f\) at \(a\) is the function whose graph is the tangent line at \((a, f(a))\): \[ L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a). \] The approximation \(f(x) \approx L(x)\) for \(x\) near \(a\) is the linear approximation (or tangent line approximation) of \(f\) at \(a\).

For example, for \(f(x) = \sqrt{x}\) at \(a = 16\): \(f(16) = 4\) and \(f'(x) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}\) gives \(f'(16) = \frac18\). So \(L(x) = 4 + \frac18(x - 16)\), and \(\sqrt{16.5} \approx L(16.5) = 4.0625\). The true value is 4.0620. Notice in Figure 6.5 that the tangent line lies above the curve, because \(\sqrt{x}\) is concave down; that is why the estimate is slightly too large. The further we move from \(a\), the worse the approximation becomes.

Differentials. The same idea in different notation: if \(y = f(x)\), we let \(dx\) be a change in \(x\) and define the differential

\[ dy = f'(x)\,dx. \]

Geometrically, \(dy\) is the rise along the tangent line, while the actual change \(\Delta y = f(x + dx) - f(x)\) is the rise along the curve (Figure 6.6). When \(dx\) is small, \(\Delta y \approx dy\). This is how we estimate errors: if a quantity \(x\) is measured with a possible error \(dx\), the resulting error in the computed \(y\) is about \(dy\). The relative error is \(\frac{dy}{y}\), and the percentage error is \(\frac{dy}{y}\times 100\%\).

Common mistake Using the linearization far from \(a\). \(L(x)\) is only reliable near the point of tangency; choose \(a\) as a "nice" number close to the value you want (16 for \(\sqrt{16.5}\), 64 for \(\sqrt[3]{63.7}\)). Also remember that \(dy\) is an estimate of \(\Delta y\), not the exact change.
Key equations
\[ L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a), \qquad f(x) \approx L(x) \text{ near } a \]
\[ dy = f'(x)\,dx, \qquad \text{relative error} = \frac{dy}{y} \]

★Key takeaways

  • Every derivative is a rate of change; its units are "units of the output per unit of the input".
  • For motion, \(v = s'\) and \(a = v'\); the particle speeds up when \(v\) and \(a\) have the same sign.
  • Total distance: split the time interval where \(v = 0\) and add the absolute changes in position.
  • Marginal cost \(C'(x)\) approximates the cost of producing one more unit.
  • Related rates: relate the quantities, differentiate with respect to \(t\), and only then substitute the numbers.
  • Near \(a\), \(f(x) \approx L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a)\); concavity tells whether the estimate is too large or too small.
  • Differentials \(dy = f'(x)\,dx\) estimate errors; relative error is \(dy/y\).

Example 6.1 A particle that turns around

A particle moves along a line with position \(s(t) = t^3 - 9t^2 + 24t\) metres, \(0 \le t \le 5\) seconds. (a) Find \(v(t)\) and \(a(t)\). (b) When is the particle at rest? (c) Find the total distance travelled in the first 5 seconds.

Similar to Stewart §2.7, Example 3
Show solution (try it first)

Given: \(s(t) = t^3 - 9t^2 + 24t\), \(0 \le t \le 5\).   Find: \(v\), \(a\), the rest times, and the total distance.

Plan: Differentiate twice for \(v\) and \(a\). Rest means \(v = 0\). For the distance, split the interval where the particle turns around and add the pieces.

Step 1. Velocity is the derivative of position:

\[ v(t) = s'(t) = 3t^2 - 18t + 24 \]

Step 2. Acceleration is the derivative of velocity:

\[ a(t) = v'(t) = 6t - 18 \]

Step 3. At rest means \(v = 0\). Factor:

\[ 3t^2 - 18t + 24 = 3(t^2 - 6t + 8) = 3(t - 2)(t - 4) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; t = 2 \text{ or } t = 4 \]

Step 4. Find the positions at the start, the turning times and the end:

\[ s(0) = 0, \quad s(2) = 8 - 36 + 48 = 20, \quad s(4) = 64 - 144 + 96 = 16, \quad s(5) = 125 - 225 + 120 = 20 \]

Step 5. Add the distances of the three pieces (forward, back, forward):

\[ |20 - 0| + |16 - 20| + |20 - 16| = 20 + 4 + 4 = 28 \text{ m} \]

Answer: (a) \(v = 3t^2 - 18t + 24\) m/s, \(a = 6t - 18\) m/s²; (b) at rest at \(t = 2\) s and \(t = 4\) s; (c) \(\boxed{28 \text{ m}}\). That is our answer.

Check: Figure 6.1 shows the peak at \((2, 20)\) and the valley at \((4, 16)\). Notice that \(s(5) - s(0) = 20\) m is only the displacement; the particle actually travelled 28 m because it went back 4 m and forward again.

Example 6.2 Marginal cost

The cost of producing \(x\) units of a product is \(C(x) = 1500 + 12x + 0.02x^2\) pesos. Find the marginal cost at \(x = 100\), interpret it, and compare it with the actual cost of producing the 101st unit.

Similar to Stewart §2.7, Example 9
Show solution (try it first)

Given: \(C(x) = 1500 + 12x + 0.02x^2\).   Find: \(C'(100)\) and \(C(101) - C(100)\).

Plan: The marginal cost is the derivative \(C'(x)\). The actual cost of one more unit is the difference \(C(101) - C(100)\).

Step 1. Differentiate term by term:

\[ C'(x) = 12 + 0.04x \]

Step 2. Evaluate at \(x = 100\):

\[ C'(100) = 12 + 0.04(100) = 16 \text{ pesos per unit} \]

Step 3. The actual cost of the 101st unit:

\[ C(101) - C(100) = \left[1500 + 1212 + 204.02\right] - \left[1500 + 1200 + 200\right] = 2916.02 - 2900 = 16.02 \]

Answer: \(\boxed{C'(100) = 16 \text{ pesos per unit}}\): when 100 units have been produced, producing one more costs about 16 pesos. The actual cost of the 101st unit is 16.02 pesos. That is our answer.

Check: The two values differ by only 0.02 pesos, as expected for a marginal cost. In Figure 6.2 the tangent line at \(x = 100\) has slope 16.

Example 6.3 Filling a conical tank

A water tank has the shape of an inverted circular cone with top radius 3 m and height 6 m. Water is pumped in at 2 m³/min. How fast is the water level rising when the water is 2 m deep?

Similar to Stewart §2.8, Example 3
Show solution (try it first)

Given: \(dV/dt = 2\) m³/min; cone with top radius 3 m and height 6 m.   Find: \(dh/dt\) when \(h = 2\) m.

Plan: Relate \(V\) and \(h\) using similar triangles to remove \(r\), then differentiate with respect to \(t\), and substitute last.

Step 1. Draw the cross-section (Figure 6.3). By similar triangles, the water radius \(r\) and depth \(h\) satisfy:

\[ \frac{r}{h} = \frac{3}{6} \;\Rightarrow\; r = \frac{h}{2} \]

Step 2. Write the volume of the water as a function of \(h\) alone:

\[ V = \frac13\pi r^2 h = \frac13\pi\left(\frac{h}{2}\right)^2 h = \frac{\pi}{12}h^3 \]

Step 3. Differentiate both sides with respect to \(t\) (Chain Rule):

\[ \frac{dV}{dt} = \frac{\pi}{12}\cdot 3h^2\frac{dh}{dt} = \frac{\pi}{4}h^2\frac{dh}{dt} \]

Step 4. Now substitute \(dV/dt = 2\) and \(h = 2\), and solve:

\[ 2 = \frac{\pi}{4}(2)^2\frac{dh}{dt} = \pi\frac{dh}{dt} \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{dh}{dt} = \frac{2}{\pi} \approx 0.637 \]

Answer: \(\boxed{\dfrac{dh}{dt} = \dfrac{2}{\pi} \approx 0.64 \text{ m/min}}\). That is our answer.

Check: Units: m³/min divided by m² gives m/min ✓. The rate is positive, so the level rises ✓. Near the narrow bottom the surface is small, so the same 2 m³/min raises the level quickly; at \(h = 4\) the rate would be only \(\frac{2}{4\pi} \approx 0.16\) m/min.

Example 6.4 The sliding ladder

A 5 m ladder leans against a vertical wall. The foot of the ladder slides away from the wall at 1 m/s. How fast is the top of the ladder sliding down the wall when the foot is 3 m from the wall?

Similar to Stewart §2.8, Example 2
Show solution (try it first)

Given: \(dx/dt = 1\) m/s, ladder length 5 m, \(x = 3\) m.   Find: \(dy/dt\) at that instant.

Plan: The Pythagorean Theorem relates \(x\) and \(y\). Differentiate with respect to \(t\), then substitute.

Step 1. Let \(x\) be the distance of the foot from the wall and \(y\) the height of the top (Figure 6.4). Then, at every moment:

\[ x^2 + y^2 = 25 \]

Step 2. Differentiate both sides with respect to \(t\):

\[ 2x\frac{dx}{dt} + 2y\frac{dy}{dt} = 0 \]

Step 3. Find \(y\) at the instant \(x = 3\):

\[ y = \sqrt{25 - 9} = 4 \]

Step 4. Substitute \(x = 3\), \(y = 4\), \(dx/dt = 1\) and solve:

\[ 2(3)(1) + 2(4)\frac{dy}{dt} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{dy}{dt} = -\frac{6}{8} = -0.75 \]

Answer: \(\boxed{\dfrac{dy}{dt} = -0.75 \text{ m/s}}\): the top slides down at 0.75 m/s. That is our answer.

Check: The negative sign means \(y\) is decreasing, as it must when the foot moves out ✓. Units m/s ✓. A tiny step: after 0.01 s, \(x = 3.01\), \(y = \sqrt{25 - 9.0601} \approx 3.99248\), a drop of 0.0075 m in 0.01 s, that is, 0.75 m/s ✓.

Example 6.5 Linear approximation

Find the linearization of \(f(x) = \sqrt{x}\) at \(a = 16\), and use it to approximate \(\sqrt{16.5}\) and \(\sqrt{15.8}\). Are the estimates too large or too small?

Similar to Stewart §2.9, Example 1
Show solution (try it first)

Given: \(f(x) = \sqrt{x}\), \(a = 16\).   Find: \(L(x)\) and the two estimates.

Plan: Compute \(f(16)\) and \(f'(16)\), write \(L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a)\), then evaluate it.

Step 1. Value and slope at \(a = 16\):

\[ f(16) = 4, \qquad f'(x) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}} \;\Rightarrow\; f'(16) = \frac{1}{8} \]

Step 2. Write the linearization:

\[ L(x) = 4 + \frac18(x - 16) \]

Step 3. Evaluate at 16.5 and at 15.8:

\[ \sqrt{16.5} \approx L(16.5) = 4 + \frac{0.5}{8} = 4.0625, \qquad \sqrt{15.8} \approx L(15.8) = 4 - \frac{0.2}{8} = 3.975 \]

Step 4. Decide the direction of the error: \(f''(x) = -\frac14 x^{-3/2} \lt 0\), so the curve is concave down and the tangent line lies above it.

Answer: \(L(x) = 4 + \frac18(x - 16)\); \(\boxed{\sqrt{16.5} \approx 4.0625}\) and \(\boxed{\sqrt{15.8} \approx 3.975}\), both slightly too large. That is our answer.

Check: A calculator gives \(\sqrt{16.5} = 4.06202\) and \(\sqrt{15.8} = 3.97492\). Both estimates are indeed a little larger, and correct to about three decimal places ✓.

Example 6.6 Error estimate with differentials

The radius of a ball bearing is measured as 21 cm, with a possible error of at most 0.05 cm. Use differentials to estimate the maximum error in the computed volume, and the percentage error.

Similar to Stewart §2.9, Example 4
Show solution (try it first)

Given: \(r = 21\) cm, \(dr = 0.05\) cm, \(V = \frac43\pi r^3\).   Find: the maximum error \(dV\) and the percentage error.

Plan: The error in \(V\) is approximated by the differential \(dV = V'(r)\,dr\). The relative error is \(dV/V\).

Step 1. Differentiate the volume formula:

\[ dV = \frac{dV}{dr}\,dr = 4\pi r^2\,dr \]

Step 2. Substitute \(r = 21\) and \(dr = 0.05\):

\[ dV = 4\pi(21)^2(0.05) = 88.2\pi \approx 277 \text{ cm}^3 \]

Step 3. Relative error. The \(\pi\) and the powers of \(r\) simplify nicely:

\[ \frac{dV}{V} = \frac{4\pi r^2\,dr}{\frac43\pi r^3} = 3\frac{dr}{r} = 3\cdot\frac{0.05}{21} \approx 0.00714 \]

Step 4. Convert to a percentage:

\[ 0.00714 \times 100\% \approx 0.71\% \]

Answer: Maximum error \(\boxed{dV \approx 277 \text{ cm}^3}\), percentage error \(\boxed{\approx 0.71\%}\). That is our answer.

Check: The volume itself is \(\frac43\pi(21)^3 \approx 38\,792\) cm³, and \(277/38\,792 \approx 0.0071\) ✓. Notice that a 0.24% error in the radius becomes three times as large (0.71%) in the volume, because the volume depends on \(r^3\).
