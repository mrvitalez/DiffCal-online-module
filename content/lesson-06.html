2.7Rates of Change in the Natural and Social Sciences

The derivative \(dy/dx\) is the instantaneous rate of change of \(y\) with respect to \(x\). Stewart §2.7 shows that this one idea appears under many different names across the sciences. In every case the recipe is the same: average rate \(\Delta y/\Delta x\) over a small interval, then the limit as \(\Delta x \to 0\). The units of the derivative are always (units of \(y\)) per (unit of \(x\)).

Physics: velocity and acceleration. If \(s = f(t)\) is the position of a particle moving on a line, then \[v(t) = \frac{ds}{dt}, \qquad a(t) = \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{d^2 s}{dt^2}.\] Take \(s = t^3 - 9t^2 + 24t\) metres. Then \(v = 3t^2 - 18t + 24 = 3(t - 2)(t - 4)\) m/s and \(a = 6t - 18\) m/s². The particle moves forward (\(v > 0\)) for \(t < 2\), backward for \(2 < t < 4\), and forward again after \(t = 4\). It is momentarily at rest at \(t = 2\) and \(t = 4\). Speed is \(|v|\). The particle is speeding up when \(v\) and \(a\) have the same sign, and slowing down when they have opposite signs.

To find the total distance travelled, split the time interval at the moments when \(v = 0\) and add the distance for each piece. Here, on \([0, 5]\): \(s(0) = 0\), \(s(2) = 20\), \(s(4) = 16\), \(s(5) = 20\), so the distance is \(20 + 4 + 4 = 28\) m, even though the final position is only 20 m from the start.

Physics: linear density. If \(m(x)\) is the mass of the first \(x\) metres of a thin rod, then \(\rho = dm/dx\) is the linear density in kg/m. For a rod with \(m = 2\sqrt{x}\) kg, \(\rho = 1/\sqrt{x}\): the rod is denser near its left end.

Chemistry: rate of reaction. If \([C]\) is the concentration (mol/L) of a product at time \(t\), the rate of reaction is \(d[C]/dt\). For example, if \([C] = \dfrac{0.5t}{1 + t}\), the Quotient Rule gives \(d[C]/dt = \dfrac{0.5}{(1+t)^2}\), which is positive but shrinking: the reaction slows down as the reactants are used up.

Biology: population growth. If \(n = f(t)\) is the size of a population, \(dn/dt\) is its growth rate (individuals per unit time). A positive rate that is itself increasing means the population is growing faster and faster.

Economics: marginal cost. If \(C(x)\) is the cost of producing \(x\) units, the marginal cost is \(C'(x)\). Since \(C(x+1) - C(x) \approx C'(x)\), it approximates the cost of producing one more unit. With \(C(x) = 1500 + 12x + 0.02x^2\) pesos, \(C'(x) = 12 + 0.04x\) and \(C'(100) = 16\) pesos per unit. The exact cost of the 101st unit is \(C(101) - C(100) = 16.02\) pesos, very close.

Common mistake Confusing a quantity with its rate. \(C'(100) = 16\) does not mean 100 units cost 16 pesos; it means the cost is increasing at about 16 pesos per extra unit at that production level. Always state the units of a derivative.
Key equations
\[ v(t) = \frac{ds}{dt}, \qquad a(t) = \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{d^2s}{dt^2} \]
\[ \rho = \frac{dm}{dx} \quad\text{(linear density)} \]
\[ C(x+1) - C(x) \approx C'(x) \quad\text{(marginal cost)} \]

2.8Related Rates

In a related rates problem, two or more quantities change with time and are tied together by an equation. You know how fast one of them changes and want the rate of another. The key tool is the Chain Rule: differentiating an equation with respect to \(t\) turns a relation between quantities into a relation between their rates. Stewart §2.8 recommends a strategy like this:

  1. Read the problem carefully and draw a diagram.
  2. Name every quantity that changes with time with a letter. Write down the rate you are given and the rate you want, as derivatives with respect to \(t\).
  3. Write an equation relating the quantities (Pythagoras, similar triangles, area or volume formulas). If necessary, use the geometry to eliminate a variable.
  4. Differentiate both sides with respect to \(t\), using the Chain Rule.
  5. Only now substitute the given values, and solve for the unknown rate.

A sliding ladder. A 5 m ladder leans against a wall. Its foot is pulled away at 0.4 m/s. How fast does the top slide down when the foot is 3 m from the wall? Let \(x\) be the distance of the foot from the wall and \(y\) the height of the top. Then \(x^2 + y^2 = 25\). Differentiate: \(2x\dfrac{dx}{dt} + 2y\dfrac{dy}{dt} = 0\). When \(x = 3\), \(y = 4\), so \[\frac{dy}{dt} = -\frac{x}{y}\frac{dx}{dt} = -\frac{3}{4}(0.4) = -0.3 \text{ m/s}.\] The top slides down at 0.3 m/s (the minus sign means \(y\) is decreasing).

A conical tank. Water flows at 2 m³/min into a tank shaped like an inverted cone with top radius 3 m and height 6 m. How fast is the water level rising when the water is 2 m deep? Let \(h\) be the depth and \(r\) the radius of the water surface. Similar triangles give \(r/h = 3/6\), so \(r = h/2\). Then \(V = \tfrac13\pi r^2 h = \dfrac{\pi h^3}{12}\), and \[\frac{dV}{dt} = \frac{\pi h^2}{4}\frac{dh}{dt} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{dh}{dt} = \frac{4(2)}{\pi(2)^2} = \frac{2}{\pi} \approx 0.64 \text{ m/min}.\]

Two moving objects. Car A is 6 km north of an intersection, driving toward it at 30 km/h. Car B is 8 km east of the intersection, driving away from it at 40 km/h. With \(y\) and \(x\) their distances from the intersection and \(z\) the distance between them, \(z^2 = x^2 + y^2\). So \(z\dfrac{dz}{dt} = x\dfrac{dx}{dt} + y\dfrac{dy}{dt}\). Now \(z = 10\), \(dx/dt = 40\) and \(dy/dt = -30\) (\(y\) is shrinking), so \(\dfrac{dz}{dt} = \dfrac{8(40) + 6(-30)}{10} = 14\) km/h.

Common mistake Substituting numbers too early. If you put \(h = 2\) into \(V = \pi h^3/12\) before differentiating, \(V\) becomes a constant and its derivative is 0. Differentiate the general equation first, then substitute. Also watch the signs: a decreasing quantity has a negative rate.
Key equations
\[ x^2 + y^2 = L^2 \;\Rightarrow\; x\frac{dx}{dt} + y\frac{dy}{dt} = 0 \]
\[ V = \tfrac13\pi r^2 h, \quad \frac{r}{h} = \text{constant (similar triangles)} \]
\[ z^2 = x^2 + y^2 \;\Rightarrow\; z\frac{dz}{dt} = x\frac{dx}{dt} + y\frac{dy}{dt} \]

2.9Linear Approximations and Differentials

A curve looks almost straight if you zoom in close to a point where it is differentiable. Stewart §2.9 uses this to approximate a complicated function near a point \(a\) by its tangent line.

Linearization The linearization of \(f\) at \(a\) is \[L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a),\] and \(f(x) \approx L(x)\) for \(x\) near \(a\) (the linear or tangent line approximation).

Example. Estimate \(\sqrt{16.5}\). Take \(f(x) = \sqrt{x}\) and \(a = 16\), a nearby number whose square root is known. Then \(f(16) = 4\) and \(f'(x) = \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}\), so \(f'(16) = \tfrac18\). Thus \(L(x) = 4 + \tfrac18(x - 16)\) and \[\sqrt{16.5} \approx L(16.5) = 4 + \tfrac18(0.5) = 4.0625.\] The true value is \(4.06202\ldots\), so the error is under 0.001. The approximation is an overestimate because \(y = \sqrt{x}\) is concave down: its tangent line lies above the curve (Figure 6.4). The further \(x\) is from \(a\), the worse the approximation becomes.

Small-angle approximation. The linearization of \(\sin x\) at \(a = 0\) is \(L(x) = 0 + \cos 0\,(x - 0) = x\). So \(\sin x \approx x\) for small \(x\) in radians, an approximation used throughout physics, for example for a swinging pendulum.

Differentials. If \(y = f(x)\), let \(dx\) be an independent variable (a small change in \(x\)) and define the differential \[dy = f'(x)\,dx.\] The true change is \(\Delta y = f(x + dx) - f(x)\). Geometrically, \(dy\) is the rise of the tangent line and \(\Delta y\) is the rise of the curve (Figure 6.5). For small \(dx\), \(\Delta y \approx dy\), and \(dy\) is much easier to compute.

Estimating errors. Suppose the radius of a ball is measured as 15 cm with a possible error of at most 0.1 cm. The volume is \(V = \tfrac43\pi r^3\), so \(dV = 4\pi r^2\,dr = 4\pi(15)^2(0.1) = 90\pi \approx 283\) cm³. That is the estimated maximum error in the computed volume. Compare it with the volume itself using the relative error: \[\frac{dV}{V} = \frac{4\pi r^2\,dr}{\tfrac43\pi r^3} = 3\,\frac{dr}{r} = 3\cdot\frac{0.1}{15} = 0.02,\] that is, a percentage error of about 2%. Notice that a 0.67% error in the radius becomes a 2% error in the volume, because volume depends on the cube of the radius.

Common mistake Using \(dx\) for the measured value. In the ball example, \(r = 15\) is where you evaluate \(f'\), and \(dr = 0.1\) is the small change. Also, \(dy\) is only an approximation of \(\Delta y\); it is not the exact change.
Key equations
\[ L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a) \]
\[ dy = f'(x)\,dx, \qquad \Delta y \approx dy \]
\[ \text{relative error} = \frac{dV}{V} \]

★Key takeaways

  • A derivative is a rate of change, and its units are (units of the output) per (unit of the input).
  • For motion on a line, v = s' and a = v' = s''; total distance needs the position at every time where v = 0.
  • Marginal cost C'(x) approximates the cost of producing one more unit.
  • In related rates, write an equation linking the quantities, differentiate with respect to t, and substitute numbers only at the end.
  • The linearization L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a) approximates f near a; it overestimates when f is concave down.
  • The differential dy = f'(x) dx estimates the true change Δy, and dV/V gives the relative (percentage) error.

Example 6.1 A particle that turns around

A particle moves on a line with position \(s(t) = 2t^3 - 15t^2 + 36t\) metres, \(t\) in seconds, \(t \ge 0\). (a) Find the velocity and acceleration. (b) When is the particle at rest? (c) Find the total distance travelled in the first 4 seconds. (d) When is it speeding up?

(Similar to Stewart §2.7, Example 1)
Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Differentiate once for velocity, twice for acceleration. The particle turns around where \(v = 0\), so split \([0, 4]\) at those times to get the distance. For (d), compare the signs of \(v\) and \(a\).

Solution:

(a) \(v(t) = 6t^2 - 30t + 36 = 6(t^2 - 5t + 6) = 6(t - 2)(t - 3)\) m/s, and \(a(t) = 12t - 30\) m/s².

(b) \(v = 0\) when \(t = 2\) s and \(t = 3\) s.

(c) Positions: \(s(0) = 0\), \(s(2) = 16 - 60 + 72 = 28\), \(s(3) = 54 - 135 + 108 = 27\), \(s(4) = 128 - 240 + 144 = 32\). Distances: \(|28 - 0| + |27 - 28| + |32 - 27| = 28 + 1 + 5 = 34\) m.

(d) Sign table:

interval\(v\)\(a\)motion
\(0 < t < 2\)+−slowing down
\(2 < t < 2.5\)−−speeding up
\(2.5 < t < 3\)−+slowing down
\(t > 3\)++speeding up

So it speeds up for \(2 < t < 2.5\) and for \(t > 3\).

Check:

The displacement is \(s(4) - s(0) = 32\) m, less than the distance 34 m, because the particle backs up 1 m between \(t = 2\) and \(t = 3\). "Speeding up" means the speed \(|v|\) grows, even when the particle moves backward, which is why \((2, 2.5)\) counts.

Example 6.2 The moving shadow

A street lamp is mounted 6 m above the ground. A person 1.5 m tall walks straight away from the lamp post at 1.2 m/s. (a) How fast is the tip of the person's shadow moving along the ground? (b) How fast is the shadow getting longer?

(Similar to Stewart §2.8, Exercises on shadows)
Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Let \(x\) be the person's distance from the post and \(s\) the length of the shadow, so the tip is at distance \(x + s\). The lamp, the top of the head and the tip of the shadow lie on one line, which gives two similar right triangles. Use them to relate \(s\) to \(x\), then differentiate with respect to \(t\). We know \(dx/dt = 1.2\).

Solution:

Similar triangles (big: height 6, base \(x + s\); small: height 1.5, base \(s\)): \[\frac{x + s}{6} = \frac{s}{1.5}.\] Multiply both sides by 6: \(x + s = 4s\), so \(s = \dfrac{x}{3}\). The tip is at \(x + s = \dfrac{4x}{3}\).

(a) \(\dfrac{d}{dt}\left(\dfrac{4x}{3}\right) = \dfrac43\dfrac{dx}{dt} = \dfrac43(1.2) = 1.6\) m/s.

(b) \(\dfrac{ds}{dt} = \dfrac13\dfrac{dx}{dt} = \dfrac13(1.2) = 0.4\) m/s.

Check:

The tip moves at the person's speed plus the rate the shadow grows: \(1.2 + 0.4 = 1.6\) m/s. Both rates are constant: they do not depend on how far away the person is. Here no numbers had to be substituted at the end, because the relation \(s = x/3\) is linear.

Example 6.3 Linear approximation and measurement error

(a) Find the linearization of \(f(x) = \sqrt[3]{x}\) at \(a = 27\) and use it to estimate \(\sqrt[3]{26.4}\). (b) The edge of a cube is measured as 12 cm with a possible error of at most 0.05 cm. Use differentials to estimate the maximum error and the percentage error in the computed volume.

(Similar to Stewart §2.9, Examples 1 and 4)
Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

(a) Choose \(a = 27\) because \(\sqrt[3]{27} = 3\) is known and 27 is close to 26.4. (b) Write \(V = x^3\), then \(dV = 3x^2\,dx\) with \(x = 12\) and \(dx = 0.05\).

Solution:

(a) \(f(x) = x^{1/3}\), \(f'(x) = \tfrac13 x^{-2/3}\). So \(f(27) = 3\) and \(f'(27) = \tfrac13\cdot\tfrac19 = \tfrac{1}{27}\). Hence \[L(x) = 3 + \frac{1}{27}(x - 27).\] With \(x = 26.4\): \(\sqrt[3]{26.4} \approx 3 + \dfrac{-0.6}{27} = 3 - 0.0222 = 2.9778\).

(b) \(dV = 3x^2\,dx = 3(12)^2(0.05) = 3(144)(0.05) = 21.6\) cm³. The volume is \(12^3 = 1728\) cm³, so the relative error is \[\frac{dV}{V} = \frac{3x^2\,dx}{x^3} = 3\,\frac{dx}{x} = 3\cdot\frac{0.05}{12} = 0.0125,\] a percentage error of about 1.25%.

Check:

(a) A calculator gives \(\sqrt[3]{26.4} = 2.97761\ldots\); the estimate 2.97778 is slightly too big, as expected since \(y = \sqrt[3]{x}\) is concave down for \(x > 0\). (b) Check: \(21.6/1728 = 0.0125\). Good.
