2.7Rates of Change in the Natural and Social Sciences

The derivative \(dy/dx\) is the instantaneous rate of change of \(y\) with respect to \(x\). Stewart §2.7 shows that this one idea appears under many different names across the sciences. In every case the recipe is the same: we take the average rate \(\Delta y/\Delta x\) over a small interval, then the limit as \(\Delta x \to 0\). Take note that the units of the derivative are always (units of \(y\)) per (unit of \(x\)).

Physics: velocity and acceleration. Recall from our physics class that if \(s = f(t)\) is the position of a particle moving on a line, then \[v(t) = \frac{ds}{dt}, \qquad a(t) = \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{d^2 s}{dt^2}.\] Let's take \(s = t^3 - 9t^2 + 24t\) metres. Then \(v = 3t^2 - 18t + 24 = 3(t - 2)(t - 4)\) m/s and \(a = 6t - 18\) m/s². We can see that the particle moves forward (\(v > 0\)) for \(t < 2\), backward for \(2 < t < 4\), and forward again after \(t = 4\). It is momentarily at rest at \(t = 2\) and \(t = 4\). Speed is \(|v|\). The particle is speeding up when \(v\) and \(a\) have the same sign, and slowing down when they have opposite signs.

How do we find the total distance travelled? We split the time interval at the moments when \(v = 0\) and add the distance for each piece. Here, on \([0, 5]\): \(s(0) = 0\), \(s(2) = 20\), \(s(4) = 16\), \(s(5) = 20\), so the distance is \(20 + 4 + 4 = 28\) m, even though the final position is only 20 m from the start (Figure 6.1).

Physics: linear density. If \(m(x)\) is the mass of the first \(x\) metres of a thin rod, then \(\rho = dm/dx\) is the linear density in kg/m. For a rod with \(m = 2\sqrt{x}\) kg, \(\rho = 1/\sqrt{x}\): the rod is denser near its left end.

Chemistry: rate of reaction. If \([C]\) is the concentration (mol/L) of a product at time \(t\), the rate of reaction is \(d[C]/dt\). For example, if \([C] = \dfrac{0.5t}{1 + t}\), the Quotient Rule gives \(d[C]/dt = \dfrac{0.5}{(1+t)^2}\). Notice that it is positive but shrinking: the reaction slows down as the reactants are used up.

Biology: population growth. If \(n = f(t)\) is the size of a population, \(dn/dt\) is its growth rate (individuals per unit time). A positive rate that is itself increasing means the population is growing faster and faster.

Economics: marginal cost. If \(C(x)\) is the cost of producing \(x\) units, the marginal cost is \(C'(x)\). Since \(C(x+1) - C(x) \approx C'(x)\), it approximates the cost of producing one more unit. With \(C(x) = 1500 + 12x + 0.02x^2\) pesos, \(C'(x) = 12 + 0.04x\) and \(C'(100) = 16\) pesos per unit. The exact cost of the 101st unit is \(C(101) - C(100) = 16.02\) pesos. Very close, right? (Figure 6.2)

Common mistake Confusing a quantity with its rate. Does \(C'(100) = 16\) mean that 100 units cost 16 pesos? No. It means the cost is increasing at about 16 pesos per extra unit at that production level. Always state the units of a derivative.
Key equations
\[ v(t) = \frac{ds}{dt}, \qquad a(t) = \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{d^2s}{dt^2} \]
\[ \rho = \frac{dm}{dx} \quad\text{(linear density)} \]
\[ C(x+1) - C(x) \approx C'(x) \quad\text{(marginal cost)} \]

2.8Related Rates

In a related rates problem, two or more quantities change with time and are tied together by an equation. We know how fast one of them changes and we want the rate of another. So, how do we get from one rate to the other? The key tool is the Chain Rule: differentiating an equation with respect to \(t\) turns a relation between quantities into a relation between their rates. Stewart §2.8 recommends a strategy like this:

  1. Read the problem carefully and draw a diagram.
  2. Name every quantity that changes with time with a letter. Write down the rate we are given and the rate we want, as derivatives with respect to \(t\).
  3. Write an equation relating the quantities (Pythagoras, similar triangles, area or volume formulas). If necessary, use the geometry to eliminate a variable.
  4. Differentiate both sides with respect to \(t\), using the Chain Rule.
  5. Only now substitute the given values, and solve for the unknown rate.

A sliding ladder. A 5 m ladder leans against a wall. Its foot is pulled away at 0.4 m/s. How fast does the top slide down when the foot is 3 m from the wall? Let \(x\) be the distance of the foot from the wall and \(y\) the height of the top (Figure 6.4). Then \(x^2 + y^2 = 25\). Let's differentiate: \(2x\dfrac{dx}{dt} + 2y\dfrac{dy}{dt} = 0\). When \(x = 3\), \(y = 4\), so \[\frac{dy}{dt} = -\frac{x}{y}\frac{dx}{dt} = -\frac{3}{4}(0.4) = -0.3 \text{ m/s}.\] The top slides down at 0.3 m/s. Take note that the minus sign means \(y\) is decreasing.

A conical tank. Water flows at 2 m³/min into a tank shaped like an inverted cone with top radius 3 m and height 6 m. How fast is the water level rising when the water is 2 m deep? Let \(h\) be the depth and \(r\) the radius of the water surface. Similar triangles give \(r/h = 3/6\), so \(r = h/2\) (Figure 6.3). Then \(V = \tfrac13\pi r^2 h = \dfrac{\pi h^3}{12}\), and \[\frac{dV}{dt} = \frac{\pi h^2}{4}\frac{dh}{dt} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{dh}{dt} = \frac{4(2)}{\pi(2)^2} = \frac{2}{\pi} \approx 0.64 \text{ m/min}.\]

Two moving objects. Car A is 6 km north of an intersection, driving toward it at 30 km/h. Car B is 8 km east of the intersection, driving away from it at 40 km/h. With \(y\) and \(x\) their distances from the intersection and \(z\) the distance between them, \(z^2 = x^2 + y^2\). So \(z\dfrac{dz}{dt} = x\dfrac{dx}{dt} + y\dfrac{dy}{dt}\). Now \(z = 10\), \(dx/dt = 40\) and \(dy/dt = -30\) (\(y\) is shrinking), so \(\dfrac{dz}{dt} = \dfrac{8(40) + 6(-30)}{10} = 14\) km/h.

Common mistake Substituting numbers too early. If we put \(h = 2\) into \(V = \pi h^3/12\) before differentiating, \(V\) becomes a constant and its derivative is 0. So we differentiate the general equation first, then substitute. Also watch the signs: a decreasing quantity has a negative rate.
Key equations
\[ x^2 + y^2 = L^2 \;\Rightarrow\; x\frac{dx}{dt} + y\frac{dy}{dt} = 0 \]
\[ V = \tfrac13\pi r^2 h, \quad \frac{r}{h} = \text{constant (similar triangles)} \]
\[ z^2 = x^2 + y^2 \;\Rightarrow\; z\frac{dz}{dt} = x\frac{dx}{dt} + y\frac{dy}{dt} \]

2.9Linear Approximations and Differentials

If we zoom in close to a point where a curve is differentiable, the curve looks almost straight. Stewart §2.9 uses this to approximate a complicated function near a point \(a\) by its tangent line. Why would we want that? Because a line is easy to compute, even when the function is not.

Linearization The linearization of \(f\) at \(a\) is \[L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a),\] and \(f(x) \approx L(x)\) for \(x\) near \(a\) (the linear or tangent line approximation).

Example. Let's estimate \(\sqrt{16.5}\). We take \(f(x) = \sqrt{x}\) and \(a = 16\), a nearby number whose square root we know. Then \(f(16) = 4\) and \(f'(x) = \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}\), so \(f'(16) = \tfrac18\). Thus \(L(x) = 4 + \tfrac18(x - 16)\) and \[\sqrt{16.5} \approx L(16.5) = 4 + \tfrac18(0.5) = 4.0625.\] The true value is \(4.06202\ldots\), so the error is under 0.001. Notice that the approximation is an overestimate. Do you know why? Because \(y = \sqrt{x}\) is concave down, its tangent line lies above the curve (Figure 6.5). The further \(x\) is from \(a\), the worse the approximation becomes.

Small-angle approximation. The linearization of \(\sin x\) at \(a = 0\) is \(L(x) = 0 + \cos 0\,(x - 0) = x\). So \(\sin x \approx x\) for small \(x\) in radians, an approximation used throughout physics, for example for a swinging pendulum.

Differentials. If \(y = f(x)\), let \(dx\) be an independent variable (a small change in \(x\)) and define the differential \[dy = f'(x)\,dx.\] The true change is \(\Delta y = f(x + dx) - f(x)\). Geometrically, \(dy\) is the rise of the tangent line and \(\Delta y\) is the rise of the curve (Figure 6.6). For small \(dx\), \(\Delta y \approx dy\), and \(dy\) is much easier to compute.

Estimating errors. Suppose the radius of a ball is measured as 15 cm with a possible error of at most 0.1 cm. How big can the error in the volume be? The volume is \(V = \tfrac43\pi r^3\), so \(dV = 4\pi r^2\,dr = 4\pi(15)^2(0.1) = 90\pi \approx 283\) cm³. That is the estimated maximum error in the computed volume. Let's compare it with the volume itself using the relative error: \[\frac{dV}{V} = \frac{4\pi r^2\,dr}{\tfrac43\pi r^3} = 3\,\frac{dr}{r} = 3\cdot\frac{0.1}{15} = 0.02,\] that is, a percentage error of about 2%. Notice that a 0.67% error in the radius becomes a 2% error in the volume, because volume depends on the cube of the radius.

Common mistake Using \(dx\) for the measured value. In the ball example, \(r = 15\) is where we evaluate \(f'\), and \(dr = 0.1\) is the small change. Also, take note that \(dy\) is only an approximation of \(\Delta y\); it is not the exact change.
Key equations
\[ L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a) \]
\[ dy = f'(x)\,dx, \qquad \Delta y \approx dy \]
\[ \text{relative error} = \frac{dV}{V} \]

★Key takeaways

  • A derivative is a rate of change, and its units are (units of the output) per (unit of the input).
  • For motion on a line, v = s' and a = v' = s''; to get the total distance, we need the position at every time where v = 0.
  • Marginal cost C'(x) approximates the cost of producing one more unit.
  • In related rates, we write an equation linking the quantities, differentiate with respect to t, and substitute numbers only at the end.
  • The linearization L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a) approximates f near a; take note that it overestimates when f is concave down.
  • The differential dy = f'(x) dx estimates the true change Δy, and dV/V gives the relative (percentage) error.

Example 6.1 A particle that turns around

A particle moves on a line with position \(s(t) = 2t^3 - 15t^2 + 36t\) metres, \(t\) in seconds, \(t \ge 0\). (a) Find the velocity and acceleration. (b) When is the particle at rest? (c) Find the total distance travelled in the first 4 seconds. (d) When is it speeding up?

(Similar to Stewart §2.7, Example 1)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • Position \(s(t) = 2t^3 - 15t^2 + 36t\) m, for \(t \ge 0\) s.
  • Find: \(v(t)\), \(a(t)\), the times at rest, the total distance on \([0, 4]\), and when the particle speeds up.
Step 1: Differentiate to get the velocity

Velocity is the derivative of position. Let's factor it too, because we will need its zeros.

\[v(t) = s'(t) = 6t^2 - 30t + 36\]

\[v(t) = 6(t^2 - 5t + 6)\]

\[v(t) = 6(t - 2)(t - 3) \text{ m/s}\]

Step 2: Differentiate again to get the acceleration

Acceleration is the derivative of velocity.

\[a(t) = v'(t) = 12t - 30 \text{ m/s}^2\]

Step 3: Find when the particle is at rest

The particle is at rest when its velocity is 0.

\[6(t - 2)(t - 3) = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad t = 2 \text{ s or } t = 3 \text{ s}\]

Step 4: Find the position at 0, 2, 3 and 4 seconds

The particle turns around at \(t = 2\) and \(t = 3\), so we need its position at those times and at the ends of \([0, 4]\).

\[s(0) = 0\]

\[s(2) = 2(8) - 15(4) + 36(2) = 16 - 60 + 72 = 28\]

\[s(3) = 2(27) - 15(9) + 36(3) = 54 - 135 + 108 = 27\]

\[s(4) = 2(64) - 15(16) + 36(4) = 128 - 240 + 144 = 32\]

Step 5: Add the distance for each piece

On each piece the particle moves in one direction only, so we add the absolute values of the changes in position.

\[|28 - 0| + |27 - 28| + |32 - 27| = 28 + 1 + 5 = 34 \text{ m}\]

Step 6: Compare the signs of v and a

The particle speeds up when \(v\) and \(a\) have the same sign. The acceleration is 0 at \(t = 2.5\), so the times 2, 2.5 and 3 split the line into pieces.

interval\(v\)\(a\)motion
\(0 < t < 2\)+−slowing down
\(2 < t < 2.5\)−−speeding up
\(2.5 < t < 3\)−+slowing down
\(t > 3\)++speeding up
Answer

(a) \(v(t) = 6(t - 2)(t - 3)\) m/s and \(a(t) = 12t - 30\) m/s². (b) At rest at \(t = 2\) s and \(t = 3\) s. (c) Total distance: 34 m. (d) It speeds up for \(2 < t < 2.5\) and for \(t > 3\).

Check

The displacement is \(s(4) - s(0) = 32\) m, less than the distance 34 m, because the particle backs up 1 m between \(t = 2\) and \(t = 3\). Does \((2, 2.5)\) really count as speeding up, even though the particle moves backward? Yes: "speeding up" means the speed \(|v|\) grows.

Example 6.2 Inflating a balloon

Air is pumped into a spherical balloon so that its volume increases at 50 cm³/s. How fast is the radius increasing when the radius is 5 cm?

(Similar to Stewart §2.8, Example 1)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • \(\dfrac{dV}{dt} = 50\) cm³/s.
  • The balloon is a sphere: \(V = \tfrac43\pi r^3\).
  • Find: \(\dfrac{dr}{dt}\) when \(r = 5\) cm.
Step 1: Write the equation that relates V and r

Both \(V\) and \(r\) change with time, and the volume formula of a sphere ties them together.

\[V = \tfrac43\pi r^3\]

Step 2: Differentiate both sides with respect to t

Since \(r\) is a function of \(t\), the Chain Rule gives a factor \(dr/dt\).

\[\frac{dV}{dt} = \tfrac43\pi\cdot 3r^2\,\frac{dr}{dt}\]

\[\frac{dV}{dt} = 4\pi r^2\,\frac{dr}{dt}\]

Step 3: Substitute the known values

Only now do we put in \(dV/dt = 50\) and \(r = 5\).

\[50 = 4\pi(5)^2\,\frac{dr}{dt}\]

\[50 = 100\pi\,\frac{dr}{dt}\]

Step 4: Solve for dr/dt

We divide both sides by \(100\pi\).

\[\frac{dr}{dt} = \frac{50}{100\pi} = \frac{1}{2\pi} \approx 0.159 \text{ cm/s}\]

Answer

The radius is increasing at \(\dfrac{1}{2\pi} \approx 0.16\) cm/s when \(r = 5\) cm.

Check

The units work: (cm³/s) divided by cm² gives cm/s. Also, take note that \(dr/dt = 50/(4\pi r^2)\) gets smaller as \(r\) grows, which makes sense: a big balloon needs more air to grow its radius by the same amount.

Example 6.3 The moving shadow

A street lamp is mounted 6 m above the ground. A person 1.5 m tall walks straight away from the lamp post at 1.2 m/s. (a) How fast is the tip of the person's shadow moving along the ground? (b) How fast is the shadow getting longer?

(Similar to Stewart §2.8, Exercises on shadows)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • Lamp height 6 m; person's height 1.5 m.
  • Let \(x\) = the person's distance from the post and \(s\) = the length of the shadow, so the tip is at distance \(x + s\).
  • \(\dfrac{dx}{dt} = 1.2\) m/s.
  • Find: \(\dfrac{d}{dt}(x + s)\) and \(\dfrac{ds}{dt}\).
Step 1: Set up similar triangles

The lamp, the top of the head and the tip of the shadow lie on one line, so the big triangle (height 6, base \(x + s\)) and the small triangle (height 1.5, base \(s\)) are similar.

\[\frac{x + s}{6} = \frac{s}{1.5}\]

Step 2: Solve for s in terms of x

Let's multiply both sides by 6, then collect the \(s\) terms.

\[x + s = \frac{6s}{1.5}\]

\[x + s = 4s\]

\[x = 3s\]

\[s = \frac{x}{3}\]

Step 3: Write the position of the tip

The tip is at distance \(x + s\) from the post.

\[x + s = x + \frac{x}{3} = \frac{4x}{3}\]

Step 4: Differentiate with respect to t

We differentiate both relations, using \(dx/dt = 1.2\).

\[\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{4x}{3}\right) = \frac43\,\frac{dx}{dt} = \frac43(1.2) = 1.6 \text{ m/s}\]

\[\frac{ds}{dt} = \frac13\,\frac{dx}{dt} = \frac13(1.2) = 0.4 \text{ m/s}\]

Answer

(a) The tip of the shadow moves at 1.6 m/s. (b) The shadow gets longer at 0.4 m/s.

Check

The tip moves at the person's speed plus the rate the shadow grows: \(1.2 + 0.4 = 1.6\) m/s. Notice that both rates are constant: they do not depend on how far away the person is. Here no numbers had to be substituted at the end, because the relation \(s = x/3\) is linear.

Example 6.4 Linear approximation and measurement error

(a) Find the linearization of \(f(x) = \sqrt[3]{x}\) at \(a = 27\) and use it to estimate \(\sqrt[3]{26.4}\). (b) The edge of a cube is measured as 12 cm with a possible error of at most 0.05 cm. Use differentials to estimate the maximum error and the percentage error in the computed volume.

(Similar to Stewart §2.9, Examples 1 and 4)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • (a) \(f(x) = x^{1/3}\), \(a = 27\) (chosen because \(\sqrt[3]{27} = 3\) and 27 is close to 26.4).
  • (b) Cube edge \(x = 12\) cm, error \(dx = 0.05\) cm, volume \(V = x^3\).
  • Find: \(L(x)\), the estimate of \(\sqrt[3]{26.4}\), \(dV\) and \(dV/V\).
Step 1: Find f(27) and f'(27)

The linearization needs the value and the slope at \(a = 27\).

\[f(27) = 27^{1/3} = 3\]

\[f'(x) = \tfrac13 x^{-2/3} = \frac{1}{3\sqrt[3]{x^2}}\]

\[f'(27) = \frac{1}{3(27)^{2/3}} = \frac{1}{3(9)} = \frac{1}{27}\]

Step 2: Write the linearization

Recall \(L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a)\).

\[L(x) = 3 + \frac{1}{27}(x - 27)\]

Step 3: Evaluate L at 26.4

We substitute \(x = 26.4\), so \(x - 27 = -0.6\).

\[L(26.4) = 3 + \frac{1}{27}(-0.6)\]

\[L(26.4) = 3 - 0.0222\]

\[L(26.4) \approx 2.9778\]

Step 4: Compute the differential dV

The differential of \(V = x^3\) estimates the change in volume caused by the measurement error.

\[dV = 3x^2\,dx\]

\[dV = 3(12)^2(0.05)\]

\[dV = 3(144)(0.05) = 21.6 \text{ cm}^3\]

Step 5: Compute the relative error

We divide by the volume \(V = 12^3 = 1728\) cm³ to compare the error with the size of the volume.

\[\frac{dV}{V} = \frac{21.6}{1728} = 0.0125\]

\[0.0125 \times 100\% = 1.25\%\]

Answer

(a) \(L(x) = 3 + \tfrac{1}{27}(x - 27)\), so \(\sqrt[3]{26.4} \approx 2.9778\). (b) The maximum error in the volume is about 21.6 cm³, a percentage error of about 1.25%.

Check

(a) A calculator gives \(\sqrt[3]{26.4} = 2.97761\ldots\); our estimate 2.97778 is slightly too big, as expected since \(y = \sqrt[3]{x}\) is concave down for \(x > 0\). (b) Another way: \(\dfrac{dV}{V} = 3\,\dfrac{dx}{x} = 3\cdot\dfrac{0.05}{12} = 0.0125\). Same answer.
