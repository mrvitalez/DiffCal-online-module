3.5Summary of Curve Sketching

A graphing calculator can draw a curve in a second, so why do we still sketch by hand? Because a screen only shows a window, and it can hide the important features: an asymptote just outside the window, a tiny wiggle, or a point where the tangent is vertical. When we put together everything from Modules 7 and 8, we can draw a graph that we understand, and that is what we need when we analyze data or a physical model. Here is the checklist we will follow.

Guidelines for sketching a curve
  1. Domain. The set of \(x\) for which \(f(x)\) is defined.
  2. Intercepts. \(y\)-intercept \(f(0)\); \(x\)-intercepts from \(f(x) = 0\) (skip if the equation is hard to solve).
  3. Symmetry. Even (\(f(-x) = f(x)\), symmetric about the \(y\)-axis), odd (\(f(-x) = -f(x)\), symmetric about the origin), or periodic.
  4. Asymptotes. Horizontal (limits at \(\pm\infty\)), vertical (where \(f \to \pm\infty\)), and slant.
  5. Intervals of increase or decrease. Sign of \(f'\).
  6. Local maximum and minimum values. First or Second Derivative Test.
  7. Concavity and points of inflection. Sign of \(f''\).
  8. Sketch the curve. Plot the key points, draw the asymptotes as dashed lines, and join everything following the shape found in E–G.

Let's run the checklist on \(f(x) = \dfrac{x}{x^2 - 4}\) (Figure 9.1). A. The domain is all \(x \ne \pm2\). B. The only intercept is \((0, 0)\). C. \(f(-x) = -f(x)\), so \(f\) is odd. D. The degree of the top is smaller, so \(y = 0\) is a horizontal asymptote; \(x = \pm 2\) are vertical asymptotes. E. By the Quotient Rule, \(f'(x) = \dfrac{(x^2 - 4) - x(2x)}{(x^2 - 4)^2} = -\dfrac{x^2 + 4}{(x^2 - 4)^2} \lt 0\), so \(f\) is decreasing on every interval of its domain. F. No local extrema. G. \(f''(x) = \dfrac{2x(x^2 + 12)}{(x^2 - 4)^3}\), which changes sign at \(x = 0\) and at the asymptotes, so \((0, 0)\) is the inflection point. H. We get three decreasing branches.

Slant asymptotes. When the degree of the numerator is exactly one more than the degree of the denominator, the graph approaches a slanted line. We find it by long division: if \(f(x) = mx + b + \dfrac{R(x)}{Q(x)}\) where the fraction goes to 0 as \(x \to \pm\infty\), then \(y = mx + b\) is a slant asymptote.

Also, take note of points where the derivative is 0 but nothing special happens. For \(f(x) = x^4 - 4x^3\) (Figure 9.2), \(f'(0) = 0\), yet the graph keeps falling through the origin. It is an inflection point with a horizontal tangent, not an extremum.

Common mistake Do not "connect the dots" across a vertical asymptote. The two sides of \(x = 2\) in Figure 9.1 belong to different branches; a line drawn across the asymptote would claim values that the function never takes.
Key equations
\[ f(x) = mx + b + \frac{R(x)}{Q(x)}, \quad \frac{R(x)}{Q(x)} \to 0 \;\Rightarrow\; y = mx + b \text{ is a slant asymptote} \]

3.7Optimization Problems

Now we are ready for the problems that made us study extreme values in the first place. "What dimensions use the least material? What is the largest area we can enclose? What is the shortest distance?" These are optimization problems. The calculus part is what we already know from Module 7; the hard part is setting up the function. So let's follow the same steps every time, just like the Given–Find–Solution habit we use in physics.

Steps in solving optimization problems
  1. Understand the problem. What is unknown? What is given? What are the conditions?
  2. Draw a diagram and label the given and the unknown quantities.
  3. Introduce a symbol for the quantity to be maximized or minimized, say \(Q\).
  4. Express \(Q\) in terms of the other variables.
  5. Reduce to one variable. Use the given conditions to eliminate the others, and state the domain of the resulting function.
  6. Find the absolute maximum or minimum with the methods of Modules 7 and 8, and justify it.

Let's see it with a classic problem: an open box is made from an 18 cm by 18 cm square sheet of cardboard by cutting equal squares of side \(x\) from the corners and folding up the sides. The base is \((18 - 2x)\) by \((18 - 2x)\) and the height is \(x\), so

\[ V(x) = x(18 - 2x)^2, \qquad 0 \le x \le 9. \]

Differentiating with the Product and Chain Rules, \(V'(x) = (18 - 2x)^2 - 4x(18 - 2x) = (18 - 2x)(18 - 6x)\). The critical numbers in the domain are \(x = 3\) and \(x = 9\). Since \(V(0) = V(9) = 0\) and \(V(3) = 3(12)^2 = 432\), the maximum volume is \(432\text{ cm}^3\) (Figure 9.3). That is our answer.

Justifying the answer. On a closed interval, the Closed Interval Method does the job. But many problems give an open or infinite domain. Then we use this:

First Derivative Test for Absolute Extreme Values Suppose \(c\) is a critical number of a continuous function \(f\) defined on an interval. (a) If \(f'(x) \gt 0\) for all \(x \lt c\) and \(f'(x) \lt 0\) for all \(x \gt c\), then \(f(c)\) is the absolute maximum value of \(f\). (b) If \(f'(x) \lt 0\) for all \(x \lt c\) and \(f'(x) \gt 0\) for all \(x \gt c\), then \(f(c)\) is the absolute minimum value of \(f\).

A useful trick for distances. The distance \(d = \sqrt{D}\) is smallest exactly when \(D = d^2\) is smallest, because the square root is an increasing function. So we minimize \(D\) and avoid differentiating a square root (Figure 9.4).

Common mistake Forgetting the domain. A critical number outside the physical domain (a negative length, or a cut larger than the sheet) must be rejected, and the endpoints of the domain must be checked. Also, answer the question that was asked: if it asks for the dimensions, giving only the maximum area is incomplete.
Key equations
\[ \text{distance}^2 = (x - x_0)^2 + (y - y_0)^2 \]
\[ \text{cylinder: } V = \pi r^2 h, \qquad A = 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi r h \]

★Key takeaways

  • Follow the checklist A–H: domain, intercepts, symmetry, asymptotes, then f′ for increase/decrease and extrema, and f″ for concavity.
  • A slant asymptote y = mx + b appears when the numerator has degree one more than the denominator; find it by long division.
  • f′(c) = 0 does not always mean an extremum; the graph can flatten and keep going.
  • In optimization, the main work is setting up one function of one variable, with its domain.
  • Justify the answer: Closed Interval Method on a closed interval, or the First Derivative Test for Absolute Extreme Values otherwise.
  • To minimize a distance, minimize its square instead.

Example 9.1 A graph with a slant asymptote

Sketch \(f(x) = \dfrac{x^2}{x - 1}\) using the checklist. Find its asymptotes and local extreme values.

(Similar to Stewart §3.5, Example 5)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: \(f(x) = \dfrac{x^2}{x - 1}\). Find: the key features for a sketch.

Plan: Go through A–G in order; use long division for the slant asymptote.

Step 1. A–C. Domain \(x \ne 1\). Intercept \((0, 0)\). \(f(-x) = \frac{x^2}{-x - 1}\) is neither \(f(x)\) nor \(-f(x)\): no symmetry.

Step 2. D. Vertical asymptote: \(x - 1 = 0\) gives \(x = 1\). Long division gives the slant asymptote.

\[\frac{x^2}{x - 1} = x + 1 + \frac{1}{x - 1} \;\Rightarrow\; y = x + 1\]

Step 3. E. Quotient Rule.

\[f'(x) = \frac{2x(x - 1) - x^2}{(x - 1)^2} = \frac{x(x - 2)}{(x - 1)^2}\]

Step 4. Sign of \(f'\): positive on \((-\infty, 0)\), negative on \((0, 1)\) and \((1, 2)\), positive on \((2, \infty)\). F. So there is a local maximum at 0 and a local minimum at 2.

\[f(0) = 0, \qquad f(2) = \frac{4}{1} = 4\]

Step 5. G. Second derivative (simplest from the long-division form \(x + 1 + (x - 1)^{-1}\)).

\[f''(x) = \frac{2}{(x - 1)^3}\]

Step 6. \(f'' \lt 0\) for \(x \lt 1\) (concave down) and \(f'' \gt 0\) for \(x \gt 1\) (concave up). No inflection point, since \(x = 1\) is not in the domain.

Answer: VA \(\boxed{x = 1}\), slant asymptote \(\boxed{y = x + 1}\), local max \(\boxed{(0, 0)}\), local min \(\boxed{(2, 4)}\).

Check: The local minimum value 4 is larger than the local maximum value 0. That is not a contradiction: they are on different branches, separated by the asymptote.

Example 9.2 The cheapest can

A cylindrical can must hold 1 L \(= 1000\text{ cm}^3\) of oil. Find the radius and height that minimize the metal used (the total surface area, with top and bottom).

(Similar to Stewart §3.7, Example 2)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: \(V = \pi r^2 h = 1000\). Find: \(r\) and \(h\) that make \(A = 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi r h\) smallest.

Plan: Use the volume condition to write \(h\) in terms of \(r\), so \(A\) becomes a function of \(r\) only; then minimize on \(r \gt 0\).

Step 1. Solve the constraint for \(h\).

\[h = \frac{1000}{\pi r^2}\]

Step 2. Substitute into the area.

\[A(r) = 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi r\cdot\frac{1000}{\pi r^2} = 2\pi r^2 + \frac{2000}{r}, \qquad r \gt 0\]

Step 3. Differentiate and set to zero.

\[A'(r) = 4\pi r - \frac{2000}{r^2} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; r^3 = \frac{500}{\pi} \;\Rightarrow\; r = \sqrt[3]{\frac{500}{\pi}} \approx 5.42\text{ cm}\]

Step 4. Justify: \(A' \lt 0\) for smaller \(r\) and \(A' \gt 0\) for larger \(r\), so this is the absolute minimum. Find \(h\); notice that \(\pi r^3 = 500\).

\[h = \frac{1000}{\pi r^2} = \frac{2(500)}{\pi r^2} = \frac{2\pi r^3}{\pi r^2} = 2r \approx 10.84\text{ cm}\]

Answer: \(\boxed{r = \sqrt[3]{500/\pi} \approx 5.42\text{ cm},\; h = 2r \approx 10.84\text{ cm}}\): the cheapest can is as tall as it is wide.

Check: \(\pi(5.42)^2(10.84) \approx 1000\text{ cm}^3\). ✓

Example 9.3 Closest point on a curve

Find the point on the curve \(y = \sqrt x\) that is closest to the point \((4, 0)\).

(Similar to Stewart §3.7, Example 3)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: points \((x, \sqrt x)\) on the curve and the fixed point \((4, 0)\). Find: the closest point.

Plan: Minimize the square of the distance, which is easier and has the same minimizer.

Step 1. Write the squared distance from \((x, \sqrt x)\) to \((4, 0)\).

\[D(x) = (x - 4)^2 + (\sqrt x - 0)^2 = x^2 - 8x + 16 + x = x^2 - 7x + 16, \quad x \ge 0\]

Step 2. Differentiate and set to zero.

\[D'(x) = 2x - 7 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; x = 3.5\]

Step 3. Justify: \(D' \lt 0\) for \(x \lt 3.5\) and \(D' \gt 0\) for \(x \gt 3.5\), so it is the absolute minimum. Find the point and the distance.

\[y = \sqrt{3.5}, \qquad d = \sqrt{D(3.5)} = \sqrt{12.25 - 24.5 + 16} = \sqrt{3.75} \approx 1.936\]

Answer: The closest point is \(\boxed{\left(3.5, \sqrt{3.5}\right) \approx (3.5, 1.871)}\), at distance \(\approx 1.936\).

Check: The endpoint \((0, 0)\) is 4 units away, which is farther. In Figure 9.4, the shortest segment meets the curve at a right angle.

Example 9.4 Fencing along a river

A farmer has 2400 m of fencing and wants to fence a rectangular field along a straight river. No fence is needed along the river. What dimensions give the largest area?

(Similar to Stewart §3.7, Example 1)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: 2400 m of fence for three sides. Find: the dimensions with maximum area.

Plan: Let \(x\) be each side perpendicular to the river and \(y\) the side parallel to it. Use the fencing condition to eliminate \(y\).

Step 1. Constraint: two sides of length \(x\) and one of length \(y\).

\[2x + y = 2400 \;\Rightarrow\; y = 2400 - 2x\]

Step 2. Area as a function of one variable, with its domain.

\[A(x) = x(2400 - 2x) = 2400x - 2x^2, \qquad 0 \le x \le 1200\]

Step 3. Differentiate and set to zero.

\[A'(x) = 2400 - 4x = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; x = 600\]

Step 4. Closed Interval Method: compare with the endpoints.

\[A(0) = 0, \qquad A(600) = 600(1200) = 720\,000, \qquad A(1200) = 0\]

Step 5. So \(x = 600\) and \(y = 2400 - 1200 = 1200\).

Answer: \(\boxed{600\text{ m} \times 1200\text{ m}}\) (the side along the river is 1200 m), giving \(720\,000\text{ m}^2\).

Check: Try a nearby choice: \(x = 500\) gives \(500 \times 1400 = 700\,000 \lt 720\,000\). ✓
