6.5Exponential Growth and Decay

Why does a population seem to grow faster and faster? Many quantities change at a rate proportional to their own size. A yeast colony with twice as many cells produces new cells twice as fast; a lump of radioactive material with half as much mass loses mass half as fast. Stewart §6.5 turns this idea into one equation: \[\frac{dy}{dt} = ky.\] This is a differential equation: an equation that involves an unknown function and its derivative. If \(k \gt 0\) it is the law of natural growth; if \(k \lt 0\) it is the law of natural decay. The constant \(k = \frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dt}\) is the relative growth rate: \(k = 0.03\) means the quantity grows by about 3% of its current size per unit of time.

Theorem The only solutions of \(dy/dt = ky\) are the exponential functions \(y(t) = y(0)\,e^{kt}\).

We can check that these work with what we learned in Module 11: if \(y = Ce^{kt}\), then \(y' = Cke^{kt} = ky\), and putting \(t = 0\) shows \(C = y(0)\). (Showing that there are no other solutions takes a little more work; Stewart does it.) Take note that every growth or decay problem comes down to finding two numbers: the starting value \(y(0)\) and the constant \(k\).

Population growth. If 800 insects grow with relative growth rate 0.05 per day, then \(P(t) = 800e^{0.05t}\), and after 10 days \(P(10) = 800e^{0.5} \approx 1319\). Usually \(k\) is not given. So, how do we find it? We use a second measurement and take natural logarithms.

Radioactive decay. The mass of a radioactive substance satisfies \(m(t) = m_0e^{kt}\) with \(k \lt 0\). The half-life \(h\) is the time it takes for half of any amount to decay. From \(\tfrac12 m_0 = m_0e^{kh}\) we get \(e^{kh} = \tfrac12\), so \(k = -\dfrac{\ln 2}{h}\), and the model can also be written \(m(t) = m_0\left(\tfrac12\right)^{t/h}\). After one half-life half remains, after two a quarter, and so on.

Newton's Law of Cooling. An object cools (or warms) at a rate proportional to the difference between its temperature \(T\) and the temperature \(T_s\) of its surroundings: \(\dfrac{dT}{dt} = k(T - T_s)\). So, how do we solve it? The trick is to let \(y = T - T_s\). Since \(T_s\) is constant, \(dy/dt = dT/dt = ky\), so \(y = y(0)e^{kt}\), that is, \(T(t) = T_s + \big(T(0) - T_s\big)e^{kt}\).

Continuously compounded interest. If \(A_0\) pesos is invested at annual rate \(r\) compounded \(n\) times a year, after \(t\) years we have \(A_0\left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt}\). What happens if we compound more and more often? Recall that \((1 + 1/m)^m \to e\) as \(m \to \infty\). Writing \(m = n/r\), the amount is \(A_0\big[(1 + 1/m)^{m}\big]^{rt}\), which approaches \(A(t) = A_0e^{rt}\) as \(n \to \infty\). This is continuous compounding, and \(dA/dt = rA\) is again the law of natural growth.

Common mistake Take note that in cooling problems the exponential describes the difference \(T - T_s\), not \(T\) itself. Writing \(T = T(0)e^{kt}\) predicts that a cup of coffee in a 25°C room cools all the way down to 0°C, which is nonsense.
Key equations
\[ \frac{dy}{dt} = ky \quad\Longrightarrow\quad y(t) = y(0)\,e^{kt} \]
\[ k = -\frac{\ln 2}{h}, \qquad m(t) = m_0\left(\tfrac12\right)^{t/h} \]
\[ T(t) = T_s + \big(T(0) - T_s\big)\,e^{kt} \]
\[ A(t) = A_0\,e^{rt} \]

6.6Inverse Trigonometric Functions

None of the six trigonometric functions is one-to-one: \(\sin x = \tfrac12\) has infinitely many solutions. So, how do we get inverses? Stewart §6.6 restricts each function to an interval where it is one-to-one and still takes all of its values.

Inverse sine. On \([-\pi/2, \pi/2]\) sine is increasing and takes every value in \([-1,1]\) exactly once. Its inverse is defined by \[\sin^{-1}x = y \iff \sin y = x \text{ and } -\tfrac{\pi}{2} \le y \le \tfrac{\pi}{2}.\] So \(\sin^{-1}x\) (also written \(\arcsin x\)) is the angle in \([-\pi/2,\pi/2]\) whose sine is \(x\). For example \(\sin^{-1}\tfrac12 = \tfrac{\pi}{6}\) and \(\sin^{-1}(-1) = -\tfrac{\pi}{2}\). The cancellation equations are \(\sin(\sin^{-1}x) = x\) for \(-1 \le x \le 1\), and \(\sin^{-1}(\sin x) = x\) only for \(-\pi/2 \le x \le \pi/2\).

Inverse cosine and inverse tangent. Cosine is restricted to \([0, \pi]\), so \(\cos^{-1}x\) is the angle in \([0,\pi]\) whose cosine is \(x\); for instance \(\cos^{-1}(-\tfrac12) = \tfrac{2\pi}{3}\). Tangent is restricted to \((-\pi/2, \pi/2)\), so \(\tan^{-1}x\) is defined for every real \(x\), its values lie in \((-\pi/2,\pi/2)\), and \(\tan^{-1}x \to \pi/2\) as \(x \to \infty\), \(\tan^{-1}x \to -\pi/2\) as \(x \to -\infty\).

Common mistake Take note that \(\sin^{-1}x\) does not mean \(\dfrac{1}{\sin x}\); that is \(\csc x = (\sin x)^{-1}\). Also, \(\sin^{-1}\big(\sin \tfrac{3\pi}{4}\big) = \tfrac{\pi}{4}\), not \(\tfrac{3\pi}{4}\), because the answer must lie in \([-\pi/2, \pi/2]\).

Compositions: draw a triangle. To simplify \(\cos(\sin^{-1}x)\), let's write \(\theta = \sin^{-1}x\), so \(\sin\theta = x\). We draw a right triangle with angle \(\theta\), opposite side \(x\) and hypotenuse 1; by Pythagoras the adjacent side is \(\sqrt{1-x^2}\). Hence \(\cos(\sin^{-1}x) = \sqrt{1-x^2}\). (The positive root is right, because \(\theta\) is in \([-\pi/2,\pi/2]\), where cosine is never negative.) In the same way \(\sec(\tan^{-1}x) = \sqrt{1+x^2}\).

Derivatives. How do we differentiate \(\sin^{-1}x\)? Let \(y = \sin^{-1}x\), so \(\sin y = x\). We differentiate implicitly: \(\cos y \,\dfrac{dy}{dx} = 1\). Using \(\cos y = \sqrt{1-x^2}\) from the triangle, \[\frac{d}{dx}\sin^{-1}x = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}}, \qquad -1 \lt x \lt 1.\] The same method with \(\tan y = x\) and \(\sec^2 y = 1 + \tan^2 y = 1 + x^2\) gives \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\tan^{-1}x = \dfrac{1}{1+x^2}\). Notice that \(\cos^{-1}x = \tfrac{\pi}{2} - \sin^{-1}x\), so the derivative of \(\cos^{-1}x\) is \(-\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\). Stewart's table also lists \(\csc^{-1}\), \(\sec^{-1}\) and \(\cot^{-1}\); for example \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\cot^{-1}x = -\dfrac{1}{1+x^2}\).

With the Chain Rule, we multiply by the inner derivative: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\tan^{-1}(5x) = \dfrac{1}{1 + (5x)^2}\cdot 5 = \dfrac{5}{1 + 25x^2}\).

Key equations
\[ \sin^{-1}x = y \iff \sin y = x,\ -\tfrac{\pi}{2} \le y \le \tfrac{\pi}{2} \]
\[ \frac{d}{dx}\sin^{-1}x = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\cos^{-1}x = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}} \]
\[ \frac{d}{dx}\tan^{-1}x = \frac{1}{1+x^2} \]
\[ \cos(\sin^{-1}x) = \sqrt{1-x^2} \]

★Key takeaways

  • If dy/dt = ky, then y(t) = y(0)e^{kt}: we get growth when k > 0 and decay when k < 0.
  • A half-life h gives k = −ln 2 / h, so the amount left after time t is m₀(1/2)^{t/h}.
  • Take note that in Newton's Law of Cooling it is the difference T − Tₛ, not T, that changes exponentially.
  • Continuous compounding at rate r turns A₀ into A₀e^{rt} after t years.
  • sin⁻¹x, cos⁻¹x and tan⁻¹x return angles in [−π/2, π/2], [0, π] and (−π/2, π/2); we draw a right triangle to simplify compositions.
  • The key derivatives are (sin⁻¹x)′ = 1/√(1 − x²), (cos⁻¹x)′ = −1/√(1 − x²) and (tan⁻¹x)′ = 1/(1 + x²).

Example 13.1 A growing bacteria culture

A bacteria culture starts with 500 cells and grows at a rate proportional to its size. After 3 hours there are 1400 cells. (a) Find a formula for the number of cells after \(t\) hours. (b) How many cells are there after 5 hours? (c) When will the population reach 5000?

(Similar to Stewart §6.5, population examples)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • \(dP/dt = kP\) with \(P(0) = 500\).
  • \(P(3) = 1400\).
  • Asked: (a) \(P(t)\); (b) \(P(5)\); (c) the time when \(P = 5000\).
Step 1: Write the general model

Growth proportional to size means \(dP/dt = kP\), so the solution is an exponential with starting value 500.

\[P(t) = P(0)e^{kt} = 500e^{kt}\]

Step 2: Use the 3-hour count to find k

We substitute \(t = 3\) and \(P = 1400\), then take \(\ln\) of both sides.

\[500e^{3k} = 1400\]

\[e^{3k} = \frac{1400}{500} = 2.8\]

\[3k = \ln 2.8\]

\[k = \tfrac13\ln 2.8 \approx 0.3432\]

Step 3: Write the formula (a)

We put \(k\) back into the model. Since \(e^{\ln 2.8} = 2.8\), the formula can also be written with base 2.8.

\[P(t) = 500e^{(\ln 2.8)t/3}\]

\[= 500\,(2.8)^{t/3}\]

Step 4: Find P(5) (b)

We substitute \(t = 5\).

\[P(5) = 500\,(2.8)^{5/3}\]

\[\approx 500(5.5624) \approx 2781\]

Step 5: Solve P(t) = 5000 (c)

We divide by 500, then take \(\ln\) of both sides to bring \(t\) down.

\[500e^{kt} = 5000\]

\[e^{kt} = 10\]

\[kt = \ln 10\]

\[t = \frac{\ln 10}{k} = \frac{3\ln 10}{\ln 2.8} \approx 6.71\]

Answer

(a) \(P(t) = 500\,(2.8)^{t/3}\). (b) About 2781 cells after 5 hours. (c) The population reaches 5000 after about 6.71 hours.

Check

\(P(3) = 500(2.8)^1 = 1400\) exactly ✓. The numbers increase in order: 1400 at 3 h, 2781 at 5 h, 5000 at about 6.7 h. Take note that we keep \(k\) exact (as \(\tfrac13 \ln 2.8\)) until the last step to avoid rounding errors.

Example 13.2 Half-life of a radioactive sample

A radioactive isotope has a half-life of 8 days. A sample starts with 200 mg. (a) How much remains after 20 days? (b) When will only 15 mg remain?

(Similar to Stewart §6.5, radioactive decay example)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • Half-life \(h = 8\) days.
  • \(m_0 = 200\) mg.
  • Asked: (a) \(m(20)\); (b) the time when \(m = 15\) mg.
Step 1: Write the model using the half-life

Every 8 days the mass is cut in half, so we use \(m(t) = m_0\left(\tfrac12\right)^{t/h}\).

\[m(t) = 200\left(\tfrac12\right)^{t/8}\]

(Equivalently \(k = -\dfrac{\ln 2}{8} \approx -0.0866\) and \(m(t) = 200e^{kt}\).)

Step 2: Find the mass after 20 days (a)

We substitute \(t = 20\). The exponent is \(20/8 = 2.5\) half-lives.

\[m(20) = 200\left(\tfrac12\right)^{20/8}\]

\[= 200\left(\tfrac12\right)^{2.5}\]

\[\approx 200(0.17678) \approx 35.36\]

Step 3: Set up the equation for 15 mg (b)

We set \(m(t) = 15\) and divide by 200.

\[200\left(\tfrac12\right)^{t/8} = 15\]

\[\left(\tfrac12\right)^{t/8} = \frac{15}{200} = \frac{3}{40}\]

Step 4: Take logarithms and solve for t

The unknown is in the exponent, so let's take \(\ln\) of both sides.

\[\frac{t}{8}\ln\tfrac12 = \ln\frac{3}{40}\]

\[\frac{t}{8}(-\ln 2) = -\ln\frac{40}{3}\]

\[t = \frac{8\ln(40/3)}{\ln 2}\]

\[\approx \frac{8(2.5903)}{0.6931} \approx 29.90\]

Answer

(a) About 35.36 mg remains after 20 days. (b) Only 15 mg remains after about 29.9 days.

Check

After 2 half-lives (16 days) there are 50 mg, and after 3 half-lives (24 days) there are 25 mg, so 35.36 mg at 20 days fits between them ✓. After 4 half-lives (32 days) there are 12.5 mg, so 15 mg at about 29.9 days, a little before 32, also makes sense ✓.

Example 13.3 Cooling soup

A bowl of soup at 90°C is placed in a room kept at 25°C. After 10 minutes the soup is at 70°C. Assuming Newton's Law of Cooling, when will the soup reach 40°C?

(Similar to Stewart §6.5, Newton's Law of Cooling)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • \(T(0) = 90\)°C, room temperature \(T_s = 25\)°C.
  • \(T(10) = 70\)°C.
  • Asked: the time when \(T = 40\)°C.
Step 1: Work with the difference y = T − 25

Newton's Law of Cooling says the difference from room temperature decays exponentially. So let's write \(y = T - 25\).

\[\frac{dy}{dt} = ky \quad\Longrightarrow\quad y(t) = y(0)e^{kt}\]

\[y(0) = 90 - 25 = 65\]

\[y(t) = 65e^{kt}\]

Step 2: Use the 10-minute reading to find k

At \(t = 10\) the soup is at 70°C, so \(y(10) = 70 - 25 = 45\).

\[65e^{10k} = 45\]

\[e^{10k} = \frac{45}{65} = \frac{9}{13}\]

\[10k = \ln\frac{9}{13}\]

\[k = \tfrac{1}{10}\ln\tfrac{9}{13} \approx -0.03677\]

Step 3: Set up the equation for 40°C

At 40°C the difference is \(y = 40 - 25 = 15\).

\[65e^{kt} = 15\]

\[e^{kt} = \frac{15}{65} = \frac{3}{13}\]

Step 4: Solve for t

We take \(\ln\) of both sides and divide by \(k\).

\[kt = \ln\frac{3}{13}\]

\[t = \frac{\ln(3/13)}{k} = \frac{10\ln(3/13)}{\ln(9/13)}\]

\[\approx \frac{10(-1.4663)}{-0.36772} \approx 39.9\]

Answer

So the soup reaches 40°C about 39.9 minutes after it is placed in the room.

Check

\(k\) is negative, as it must be for cooling ✓. The first 20 degrees took 10 minutes, but the next 30 degrees took about 30 more minutes. Does that make sense? Yes: cooling slows down as the soup approaches room temperature, which matches Figure 13.2. Take note that using \(T\) instead of \(T - 25\) would give \(90e^{10k} = 70\) and a wrong time.

Example 13.4 A derivative that simplifies, and a tangent line

Let \(f(x) = x\sin^{-1}x + \sqrt{1 - x^2}\) for \(-1 \lt x \lt 1\). (a) Find \(f'(x)\) and simplify. (b) Find an equation of the tangent line to \(y = f(x)\) at \(x = \tfrac12\).

(Similar to Stewart §6.6, derivative examples)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • \(f(x) = x\sin^{-1}x + \sqrt{1 - x^2}\), \(-1 \lt x \lt 1\).
  • Asked: (a) \(f'(x)\), simplified; (b) the tangent line at \(x = \tfrac12\).
Step 1: Differentiate x sin⁻¹x with the Product Rule

The first term is a product, so we use \((uv)' = u'v + uv'\) with \(u = x\) and \(v = \sin^{-1}x\).

\[\frac{d}{dx}\big[x\sin^{-1}x\big] = (1)\sin^{-1}x + x\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\]

\[= \sin^{-1}x + \frac{x}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\]

Step 2: Differentiate √(1 − x²) with the Chain Rule

We write the root as a power, then multiply by the inner derivative \(-2x\).

\[\frac{d}{dx}(1-x^2)^{1/2} = \tfrac12(1-x^2)^{-1/2}\cdot(-2x)\]

\[= -\frac{x}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\]

Step 3: Add the two results (a)

Notice that the two fractions are opposites, so they cancel.

\[f'(x) = \sin^{-1}x + \frac{x}{\sqrt{1-x^2}} - \frac{x}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\]

\[= \sin^{-1}x\]

Step 4: Find the slope at x = 1/2

We substitute into \(f'\). Recall that \(\sin^{-1}\tfrac12\) is the angle in \([-\pi/2, \pi/2]\) whose sine is \(\tfrac12\).

\[m = f'\!\left(\tfrac12\right) = \sin^{-1}\tfrac12 = \frac{\pi}{6}\]

Step 5: Find the point of tangency

We substitute \(x = \tfrac12\) into \(f\).

\[f\!\left(\tfrac12\right) = \tfrac12\cdot\frac{\pi}{6} + \sqrt{1 - \tfrac14}\]

\[= \frac{\pi}{12} + \sqrt{\tfrac34}\]

\[= \frac{\pi}{12} + \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\]

Step 6: Write the tangent line (b)

We use the point-slope form \(y - y_1 = m(x - x_1)\), then simplify.

\[y - \left(\frac{\pi}{12} + \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right) = \frac{\pi}{6}\left(x - \frac12\right)\]

\[y = \frac{\pi}{6}x - \frac{\pi}{12} + \frac{\pi}{12} + \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\]

\[y = \frac{\pi}{6}x + \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\]

Answer

(a) \(f'(x) = \sin^{-1}x\). (b) The tangent line is \(y = \dfrac{\pi}{6}x + \dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\).

Check

At \(x = \tfrac12\) the line gives \(\tfrac{\pi}{12} + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\), the same as \(f(\tfrac12)\) ✓. Take note that \(\sin^{-1}\tfrac12 = \tfrac{\pi}{6} \approx 0.524\), not 30: in calculus, angles are measured in radians.
