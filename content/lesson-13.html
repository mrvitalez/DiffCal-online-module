6.5Exponential Growth and Decay

Many quantities change at a rate proportional to their own size. A yeast colony with twice as many cells produces new cells twice as fast; a lump of radioactive material with half as much mass loses mass half as fast. Stewart §6.5 turns this idea into one equation: \[\frac{dy}{dt} = ky.\] This is a differential equation: an equation that involves an unknown function and its derivative. If \(k \gt 0\) it is the law of natural growth; if \(k \lt 0\) it is the law of natural decay. The constant \(k = \frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dt}\) is the relative growth rate: \(k = 0.03\) means the quantity grows by about 3% of its current size per unit of time.

Theorem The only solutions of \(dy/dt = ky\) are the exponential functions \(y(t) = y(0)\,e^{kt}\).

You can check that these work with what you learned in Module 11: if \(y = Ce^{kt}\), then \(y' = Cke^{kt} = ky\), and putting \(t = 0\) shows \(C = y(0)\). (Showing that there are no other solutions takes a little more work; Stewart does it.) So every growth or decay problem comes down to finding two numbers: the starting value \(y(0)\) and the constant \(k\).

Population growth. If 800 insects grow with relative growth rate 0.05 per day, then \(P(t) = 800e^{0.05t}\), and after 10 days \(P(10) = 800e^{0.5} \approx 1319\). Usually \(k\) is not given; you find it from a second measurement by taking natural logarithms.

Radioactive decay. The mass of a radioactive substance satisfies \(m(t) = m_0e^{kt}\) with \(k \lt 0\). The half-life \(h\) is the time it takes for half of any amount to decay. From \(\tfrac12 m_0 = m_0e^{kh}\) you get \(e^{kh} = \tfrac12\), so \(k = -\dfrac{\ln 2}{h}\), and the model can also be written \(m(t) = m_0\left(\tfrac12\right)^{t/h}\). After one half-life half remains, after two a quarter, and so on.

Newton's Law of Cooling. An object cools (or warms) at a rate proportional to the difference between its temperature \(T\) and the temperature \(T_s\) of its surroundings: \(\dfrac{dT}{dt} = k(T - T_s)\). The trick is to let \(y = T - T_s\). Since \(T_s\) is constant, \(dy/dt = dT/dt = ky\), so \(y = y(0)e^{kt}\), that is, \(T(t) = T_s + \big(T(0) - T_s\big)e^{kt}\).

Continuously compounded interest. If \(A_0\) pesos is invested at annual rate \(r\) compounded \(n\) times a year, after \(t\) years you have \(A_0\left(1 + \frac{r}{n}\right)^{nt}\). Recall that \((1 + 1/m)^m \to e\) as \(m \to \infty\). Writing \(m = n/r\), the amount is \(A_0\big[(1 + 1/m)^{m}\big]^{rt}\), which approaches \(A(t) = A_0e^{rt}\) as \(n \to \infty\). This is continuous compounding, and \(dA/dt = rA\) is again the law of natural growth.

Common mistake In cooling problems the exponential describes the difference \(T - T_s\), not \(T\) itself. Writing \(T = T(0)e^{kt}\) predicts that a cup of coffee in a 25°C room cools all the way down to 0°C, which is nonsense.
Key equations
\[ \frac{dy}{dt} = ky \quad\Longrightarrow\quad y(t) = y(0)\,e^{kt} \]
\[ k = -\frac{\ln 2}{h}, \qquad m(t) = m_0\left(\tfrac12\right)^{t/h} \]
\[ T(t) = T_s + \big(T(0) - T_s\big)\,e^{kt} \]
\[ A(t) = A_0\,e^{rt} \]

6.6Inverse Trigonometric Functions

None of the six trigonometric functions is one-to-one: \(\sin x = \tfrac12\) has infinitely many solutions. To get inverses, Stewart §6.6 restricts each function to an interval where it is one-to-one and still takes all of its values.

Inverse sine. On \([-\pi/2, \pi/2]\) sine is increasing and takes every value in \([-1,1]\) exactly once. Its inverse is defined by \[\sin^{-1}x = y \iff \sin y = x \text{ and } -\tfrac{\pi}{2} \le y \le \tfrac{\pi}{2}.\] So \(\sin^{-1}x\) (also written \(\arcsin x\)) is the angle in \([-\pi/2,\pi/2]\) whose sine is \(x\). For example \(\sin^{-1}\tfrac12 = \tfrac{\pi}{6}\) and \(\sin^{-1}(-1) = -\tfrac{\pi}{2}\). The cancellation equations are \(\sin(\sin^{-1}x) = x\) for \(-1 \le x \le 1\), and \(\sin^{-1}(\sin x) = x\) only for \(-\pi/2 \le x \le \pi/2\).

Inverse cosine and inverse tangent. Cosine is restricted to \([0, \pi]\), so \(\cos^{-1}x\) is the angle in \([0,\pi]\) whose cosine is \(x\); for instance \(\cos^{-1}(-\tfrac12) = \tfrac{2\pi}{3}\). Tangent is restricted to \((-\pi/2, \pi/2)\), so \(\tan^{-1}x\) is defined for every real \(x\), its values lie in \((-\pi/2,\pi/2)\), and \(\tan^{-1}x \to \pi/2\) as \(x \to \infty\), \(\tan^{-1}x \to -\pi/2\) as \(x \to -\infty\).

Common mistake \(\sin^{-1}x\) does not mean \(\dfrac{1}{\sin x}\); that is \(\csc x = (\sin x)^{-1}\). Also, \(\sin^{-1}\big(\sin \tfrac{3\pi}{4}\big) = \tfrac{\pi}{4}\), not \(\tfrac{3\pi}{4}\), because the answer must lie in \([-\pi/2, \pi/2]\).

Compositions: draw a triangle. To simplify \(\cos(\sin^{-1}x)\), let \(\theta = \sin^{-1}x\), so \(\sin\theta = x\). Draw a right triangle with angle \(\theta\), opposite side \(x\) and hypotenuse 1; by Pythagoras the adjacent side is \(\sqrt{1-x^2}\). Hence \(\cos(\sin^{-1}x) = \sqrt{1-x^2}\). (The positive root is right, because \(\theta\) is in \([-\pi/2,\pi/2]\), where cosine is never negative.) In the same way \(\sec(\tan^{-1}x) = \sqrt{1+x^2}\).

Derivatives. Let \(y = \sin^{-1}x\), so \(\sin y = x\). Differentiate implicitly: \(\cos y \,\dfrac{dy}{dx} = 1\). Using \(\cos y = \sqrt{1-x^2}\) from the triangle, \[\frac{d}{dx}\sin^{-1}x = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}}, \qquad -1 \lt x \lt 1.\] The same method with \(\tan y = x\) and \(\sec^2 y = 1 + \tan^2 y = 1 + x^2\) gives \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\tan^{-1}x = \dfrac{1}{1+x^2}\). Because \(\cos^{-1}x = \tfrac{\pi}{2} - \sin^{-1}x\), the derivative of \(\cos^{-1}x\) is \(-\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\). Stewart's table also lists \(\csc^{-1}\), \(\sec^{-1}\) and \(\cot^{-1}\); for example \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\cot^{-1}x = -\dfrac{1}{1+x^2}\).

With the Chain Rule: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\tan^{-1}(5x) = \dfrac{1}{1 + (5x)^2}\cdot 5 = \dfrac{5}{1 + 25x^2}\).

Key equations
\[ \sin^{-1}x = y \iff \sin y = x,\ -\tfrac{\pi}{2} \le y \le \tfrac{\pi}{2} \]
\[ \frac{d}{dx}\sin^{-1}x = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\cos^{-1}x = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}} \]
\[ \frac{d}{dx}\tan^{-1}x = \frac{1}{1+x^2} \]
\[ \cos(\sin^{-1}x) = \sqrt{1-x^2} \]

★Key takeaways

  • If dy/dt = ky, then y(t) = y(0)e^{kt}: growth when k > 0 and decay when k < 0.
  • A half-life h gives k = −ln 2 / h, so the amount left after time t is m₀(1/2)^{t/h}.
  • In Newton's Law of Cooling it is the difference T − Tₛ, not T, that changes exponentially.
  • Continuous compounding at rate r turns A₀ into A₀e^{rt} after t years.
  • sin⁻¹x, cos⁻¹x and tan⁻¹x return angles in [−π/2, π/2], [0, π] and (−π/2, π/2); a right triangle simplifies compositions.
  • The key derivatives are (sin⁻¹x)′ = 1/√(1 − x²), (cos⁻¹x)′ = −1/√(1 − x²) and (tan⁻¹x)′ = 1/(1 + x²).

Example 13.1 A growing bacteria culture

A bacteria culture starts with 500 cells and grows at a rate proportional to its size. After 3 hours there are 1400 cells. (a) Find a formula for the number of cells after \(t\) hours. (b) How many cells are there after 5 hours? (c) When will the population reach 5000?

(Similar to Stewart §6.5, population examples)
Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Growth proportional to size means \(dP/dt = kP\), so \(P(t) = P(0)e^{kt} = 500e^{kt}\). Use the 3-hour count to find \(k\), then use the formula. Solving for a time means taking \(\ln\) of both sides.

Solution:

(a) At \(t = 3\): \(500e^{3k} = 1400\), so \(e^{3k} = 2.8\). Take \(\ln\): \(3k = \ln 2.8\), so \(k = \tfrac13\ln 2.8 \approx 0.3432\). Thus \[P(t) = 500e^{(\ln 2.8)t/3} = 500\,(2.8)^{t/3}.\]

(b) \(P(5) = 500\,(2.8)^{5/3} \approx 2781\) cells.

(c) Solve \(500e^{kt} = 5000\): \(e^{kt} = 10\), so \(kt = \ln 10\) and \[t = \frac{\ln 10}{k} = \frac{3\ln 10}{\ln 2.8} \approx 6.71 \text{ hours}.\]

Check:

The numbers are consistent: 1400 at 3 h, 2781 at 5 h and 5000 at about 6.7 h, an increasing sequence. Also \(P(3) = 500(2.8)^1 = 1400\) exactly. Keep \(k\) exact (as \(\tfrac13 \ln 2.8\)) until the last step to avoid rounding errors.

Example 13.2 Cooling soup

A bowl of soup at 90°C is placed in a room kept at 25°C. After 10 minutes the soup is at 70°C. Assuming Newton's Law of Cooling, when will the soup reach 40°C?

(Similar to Stewart §6.5, Newton's Law of Cooling)
Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Let \(y = T - 25\), the amount by which the soup is hotter than the room. Then \(dy/dt = ky\), so \(y = y(0)e^{kt}\) with \(y(0) = 90 - 25 = 65\). Use the 10-minute reading to find \(k\).

Solution:

At \(t = 10\): \(y(10) = 70 - 25 = 45\), so \(65e^{10k} = 45\), \(e^{10k} = \tfrac{9}{13}\), and \(k = \tfrac{1}{10}\ln\tfrac{9}{13} \approx -0.03677\).

The soup is at 40°C when \(y = 40 - 25 = 15\): \[65e^{kt} = 15 \;\Longrightarrow\; e^{kt} = \tfrac{3}{13} \;\Longrightarrow\; t = \frac{\ln(3/13)}{k} = \frac{10\ln(3/13)}{\ln(9/13)} \approx 39.9 \text{ min}.\]

Check:

\(k\) is negative, as it must be for cooling. The first 20 degrees took 10 minutes but the next 30 degrees took about 30 more minutes: cooling slows down as the soup approaches room temperature, which matches Figure 13.2. A common error is to use \(T\) instead of \(T - 25\), which would give \(90e^{10k} = 70\) and a wrong time.

Example 13.3 A derivative that simplifies, and a tangent line

Let \(f(x) = x\sin^{-1}x + \sqrt{1 - x^2}\) for \(-1 \lt x \lt 1\). (a) Find \(f'(x)\) and simplify. (b) Find an equation of the tangent line to \(y = f(x)\) at \(x = \tfrac12\).

(Similar to Stewart §6.6, derivative examples)
Show solution (try it first)

Strategy:

Use the Product Rule on \(x\sin^{-1}x\) and the Chain Rule on \(\sqrt{1 - x^2} = (1-x^2)^{1/2}\). For (b) you need the point \(\big(\tfrac12, f(\tfrac12)\big)\) and the slope \(f'(\tfrac12)\).

Solution:

(a) Product Rule: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\big[x\sin^{-1}x\big] = 1\cdot\sin^{-1}x + x\cdot\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\).

Chain Rule: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}(1-x^2)^{1/2} = \tfrac12(1-x^2)^{-1/2}\cdot(-2x) = -\dfrac{x}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\).

Adding, the two fractions cancel: \[f'(x) = \sin^{-1}x + \frac{x}{\sqrt{1-x^2}} - \frac{x}{\sqrt{1-x^2}} = \sin^{-1}x.\]

(b) Slope: \(f'(\tfrac12) = \sin^{-1}\tfrac12 = \tfrac{\pi}{6}\). Point: \(f(\tfrac12) = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{\pi}{6} + \sqrt{1 - \tfrac14} = \tfrac{\pi}{12} + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\). Tangent line: \[y = \frac{\pi}{12} + \frac{\sqrt3}{2} + \frac{\pi}{6}\left(x - \frac12\right) = \frac{\pi}{6}x + \frac{\sqrt3}{2}.\]

Check:

Remember that \(\sin^{-1}\tfrac12\) is the angle in \([-\pi/2,\pi/2]\) whose sine is \(\tfrac12\), namely \(\tfrac{\pi}{6} \approx 0.524\), not 30: in calculus, angles are always measured in radians. The neat result \(f' = \sin^{-1}x\) is worth remembering: you will see this function again when you study integrals.
