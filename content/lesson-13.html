6.5Exponential Growth and Decay

Let's think about a population of bacteria. If there are twice as many bacteria, there are twice as many cells dividing, so the population grows twice as fast. In other words, the rate of growth is proportional to the size. The same idea describes radioactive atoms decaying, a hot object cooling, and money earning interest. So the question for this section is: what function has a rate of change proportional to itself?

Mathematically, if \(y(t)\) is the quantity at time \(t\), we are saying \[\frac{dy}{dt} = ky\] for some constant \(k\). This is called a differential equation, because it is an equation involving a derivative. If \(k > 0\) it is the law of natural growth; if \(k < 0\) it is the law of natural decay.

Theorem The only solutions of the differential equation \(\dfrac{dy}{dt} = ky\) are the exponential functions \[y(t) = y(0)\,e^{kt}.\]

We can check it directly: if \(y = y(0)e^{kt}\), then \(\dfrac{dy}{dt} = y(0)e^{kt}\cdot k = ky\). (Recall the Chain Rule from Module 11.) Notice that the formula has only two unknowns: the starting value \(y(0)\) and the rate \(k\). So in every problem, we need two pieces of information to find them.

Population growth. In biology, \(k\) is called the relative growth rate, because \(k = \dfrac{1}{P}\dfrac{dP}{dt}\) is the growth rate as a fraction of the population. If \(k = 0.02\) per year, the population grows by about 2% of its size every year.

Radioactive decay. Radioactive atoms decay at a rate proportional to the mass present, so \(m(t) = m(0)e^{kt}\) with \(k < 0\). Physicists describe the rate with the half-life \(h\), the time for half of any amount to decay. Setting \(m(h) = \tfrac12 m(0)\) gives \(e^{kh} = \tfrac12\), so \[k = -\frac{\ln 2}{h}, \qquad\text{and equivalently}\qquad m(t) = m(0)\,2^{-t/h}.\] Take note of how simple the second form is: after one half-life we have \(\tfrac12\), after two half-lives \(\tfrac14\), after three \(\tfrac18\) (Figure 13.1). Carbon-14 dating, and the ages of meteorites from uranium and lead, are based on exactly this formula.

Newton's Law of Cooling. A hot object in a room cools at a rate proportional to the difference between its temperature and the room temperature \(T_s\): \[\frac{dT}{dt} = k(T - T_s).\] This is not quite our equation, but if we let \(y = T - T_s\), then \(\dfrac{dy}{dt} = \dfrac{dT}{dt} = ky\). So \(y = y(0)e^{kt}\), that is, \[T(t) = T_s + (T_0 - T_s)\,e^{kt}.\] The difference from room temperature decays exponentially, so the object approaches the room temperature (Figure 13.2).

Continuously compounded interest. If \(A_0\) is invested at an annual interest rate \(r\) compounded \(n\) times a year, after \(t\) years we have \(A_0\left(1 + \dfrac{r}{n}\right)^{nt}\). Recall from Module 12 that \(\left(1 + \dfrac1m\right)^m \to e\). Letting \(n \to \infty\), \[A(t) = A_0\,e^{rt},\] which satisfies \(\dfrac{dA}{dt} = rA\): the money grows at a rate proportional to itself.

How to solve a growth or decay problem. (1) Write the model \(y = y(0)e^{kt}\) (or the cooling version). (2) Use the given data to find \(y(0)\) and \(k\); you will need a logarithm to get \(k\) out of the exponent. (3) Answer the question: either substitute a time, or set \(y\) equal to a value and solve for \(t\) with \(\ln\).

Common mistake In Newton's Law of Cooling, the exponential describes \(T - T_s\), not \(T\). Writing \(T = T_0e^{kt}\) would make the soup cool all the way to 0°C instead of to room temperature. Also, keep the full value of \(k\) (or keep it exact like \(\tfrac{\ln 2.8}{3}\)); rounding \(k\) early can throw off the final answer.
Key equations
\[ \frac{dy}{dt} = ky \;\Longrightarrow\; y(t) = y(0)\,e^{kt} \]
\[ m(t) = m(0)\,2^{-t/h}, \qquad k = -\frac{\ln 2}{h} \]
\[ T(t) = T_s + (T_0 - T_s)e^{kt}, \qquad A(t) = A_0e^{rt} \]

6.6Inverse Trigonometric Functions

In Module 11 we learned that only one-to-one functions have inverses. But the trigonometric functions are periodic: \(\sin x = \tfrac12\) at \(x = \tfrac{\pi}{6}, \tfrac{5\pi}{6}, \tfrac{13\pi}{6}, \ldots\). They fail the Horizontal Line Test badly. So how can we "undo" sine? Again, we restrict the domain to a piece where the function is one-to-one.

Definition Restrict sine to \(\left[-\tfrac{\pi}{2}, \tfrac{\pi}{2}\right]\), cosine to \([0, \pi]\) and tangent to \(\left(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}, \tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)\). Then \[\sin^{-1}x = y \iff \sin y = x \;\text{ and }\; -\tfrac{\pi}{2} \le y \le \tfrac{\pi}{2}, \qquad -1 \le x \le 1,\] \[\cos^{-1}x = y \iff \cos y = x \;\text{ and }\; 0 \le y \le \pi, \qquad -1 \le x \le 1,\] \[\tan^{-1}x = y \iff \tan y = x \;\text{ and }\; -\tfrac{\pi}{2} < y < \tfrac{\pi}{2}, \qquad x \text{ any real number}.\]

We also write \(\arcsin x\), \(\arccos x\), \(\arctan x\). In words, \(\sin^{-1}x\) is "the angle in \(\left[-\tfrac{\pi}{2}, \tfrac{\pi}{2}\right]\) whose sine is \(x\)." For example, \(\sin^{-1}\tfrac12 = \tfrac{\pi}{6}\), \(\cos^{-1}(-1) = \pi\), and \(\tan^{-1}1 = \tfrac{\pi}{4}\). The graph of \(\sin^{-1}\) is the reflection of the restricted sine about \(y = x\) (Figure 13.3). As \(x \to \pm\infty\), \(\tan^{-1}x \to \pm\tfrac{\pi}{2}\), so \(\tan^{-1}\) has two horizontal asymptotes (Figure 13.4). In astronomy, inverse trig is how we turn a measured ratio back into an angle, like an altitude or a parallax angle.

Common mistake The cancellation equation \(\sin^{-1}(\sin x) = x\) is only true for \(-\tfrac{\pi}{2} \le x \le \tfrac{\pi}{2}\). For example, \(\sin^{-1}\!\left(\sin\tfrac{5\pi}{6}\right) = \sin^{-1}\tfrac12 = \tfrac{\pi}{6}\), not \(\tfrac{5\pi}{6}\). Also, \(\sin^{-1}x\) is not \(\dfrac{1}{\sin x}\); that is \(\csc x\).

Compositions with a triangle. To simplify something like \(\cos(\sin^{-1}x)\), let \(\theta = \sin^{-1}x\), so \(\sin\theta = x = \dfrac{x}{1}\). Draw a right triangle with opposite side \(x\) and hypotenuse 1; by Pythagoras the adjacent side is \(\sqrt{1 - x^2}\). Then \(\cos(\sin^{-1}x) = \cos\theta = \sqrt{1 - x^2}\). (It is positive because \(\theta\) is in \(\left[-\tfrac{\pi}{2}, \tfrac{\pi}{2}\right]\), where cosine is not negative.)

Derivatives. Let's derive the derivative of \(y = \sin^{-1}x\). It means \(\sin y = x\) with \(-\tfrac{\pi}{2} \le y \le \tfrac{\pi}{2}\). Differentiate implicitly: \[\cos y\,\frac{dy}{dx} = 1 \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{\cos y} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \sin^2 y}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}}.\] (We used \(\cos y \ge 0\) on this interval, so \(\cos y = +\sqrt{1 - \sin^2 y}\).) In the same way, from \(\tan y = x\): \(\sec^2 y\,y' = 1\), so \(y' = \dfrac{1}{1 + \tan^2 y} = \dfrac{1}{1 + x^2}\).

Derivatives of inverse trigonometric functions \[\frac{d}{dx}\sin^{-1}x = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\cos^{-1}x = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\tan^{-1}x = \frac{1}{1 + x^2}.\]

Take note that these derivatives are algebraic functions, with no trig left in them. That is why inverse trig functions will appear so often as antiderivatives in Integral Calculus. With the Chain Rule, for example, \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\tan^{-1}(3x) = \dfrac{3}{1 + 9x^2}\) and \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin^{-1}(e^x) = \dfrac{e^x}{\sqrt{1 - e^{2x}}}\).

Key equations
\[ \sin^{-1}x = y \iff \sin y = x,\; -\tfrac{\pi}{2} \le y \le \tfrac{\pi}{2} \]
\[ \frac{d}{dx}\sin^{-1}x = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\tan^{-1}x = \frac{1}{1 + x^2} \]

★Key takeaways

  • If a quantity changes at a rate proportional to itself, dy/dt = ky, then y = y(0)e^(kt). Two pieces of data determine y(0) and k.
  • Half-life h: m(t) = m(0)·2^(−t/h). After every half-life, half of what is left remains.
  • Newton's Law of Cooling: T − Tₛ decays exponentially, so T(t) = Tₛ + (T₀ − Tₛ)e^(kt) approaches room temperature.
  • Continuous compounding: A = A₀e^(rt).
  • sin⁻¹, cos⁻¹, tan⁻¹ undo the trig functions on restricted domains; sin⁻¹(sin x) = x only for x in [−π/2, π/2].
  • d/dx sin⁻¹x = 1/√(1 − x²), d/dx cos⁻¹x = −1/√(1 − x²), d/dx tan⁻¹x = 1/(1 + x²).

Example 13.1 A growing bacteria culture

A bacteria culture starts with 500 cells and grows at a rate proportional to its size. After 3 hours there are 1400 cells. (a) Find a formula for the number of cells after \(t\) hours. (b) How many cells are there after 5 hours? (c) When will the population reach 5000?

(Similar to Stewart §6.5, population examples)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: \(P(0) = 500\), \(P(3) = 1400\), \(\dfrac{dP}{dt} = kP\).   Find: (a) \(P(t)\); (b) \(P(5)\); (c) \(t\) when \(P = 5000\).

Plan: The model is \(P = P(0)e^{kt}\). Use the second data point to find \(k\) (take ln to bring it down), then substitute or solve.

Step 1. Write the model with the starting value:

\[P(t) = 500e^{kt}\]

Step 2. Use \(P(3) = 1400\) and divide by 500:

\[500e^{3k} = 1400 \;\Longrightarrow\; e^{3k} = 2.8\]

Step 3. Take \(\ln\) of both sides to find \(k\):

\[3k = \ln 2.8 \;\Longrightarrow\; k = \frac{\ln 2.8}{3} \approx 0.3432 \text{ per hour}\]

Step 4. (b) Substitute \(t = 5\) (keep \(k\) exact):

\[P(5) = 500e^{5\ln(2.8)/3} = 500(2.8)^{5/3} \approx 2781\]

Step 5. (c) Set \(P(t) = 5000\) and solve for \(t\):

\[500e^{kt} = 5000 \;\Longrightarrow\; e^{kt} = 10 \;\Longrightarrow\; t = \frac{\ln 10}{k} = \frac{3\ln 10}{\ln 2.8} \approx 6.71 \text{ h}\]

Answer: (a) \(\boxed{P(t) = 500e^{(\ln 2.8/3)t} \approx 500e^{0.3432t}}\); (b) about \(\boxed{2781}\) cells; (c) after about \(\boxed{6.71}\) hours. That is our answer.

Check: The population is multiplied by 2.8 every 3 hours. Five hours is a bit less than two of those periods, and \(500(2.8)^2 = 3920\), so about 2781 at 5 h is reasonable; 5000 is reached a little after 6 h. ✓

Example 13.2 Half-life of radium

Radium-226 has a half-life of 1590 years. A sample has a mass of 100 mg. (a) Find the mass after 1000 years. (b) When will the mass be reduced to 30 mg?

(Similar to Stewart §6.5, radioactive decay)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: \(m(0) = 100\) mg, half-life \(h = 1590\) years.   Find: (a) \(m(1000)\); (b) \(t\) when \(m = 30\).

Plan: Use the half-life form \(m = m(0)2^{-t/h}\); for (b), take ln of both sides to bring \(t\) down.

Step 1. Write the model:

\[m(t) = 100\cdot 2^{-t/1590}\]

Step 2. (a) Substitute \(t = 1000\):

\[m(1000) = 100\cdot 2^{-1000/1590} = 100\cdot 2^{-0.6289} \approx 64.7 \text{ mg}\]

Step 3. (b) Set \(m = 30\) and divide by 100:

\[2^{-t/1590} = 0.3\]

Step 4. Take \(\ln\) of both sides, and use \(\ln(2^u) = u\ln 2\):

\[-\frac{t}{1590}\ln 2 = \ln 0.3 \;\Longrightarrow\; t = -\frac{1590\ln 0.3}{\ln 2} \approx 2762 \text{ years}\]

Answer: (a) about \(\boxed{64.7 \text{ mg}}\); (b) after about \(\boxed{2762 \text{ years}}\). That is our answer.

Check: 1000 years is less than one half-life, so more than 50 mg should remain: 64.7 mg ✓. 30 mg is between 50 mg (1 half-life) and 25 mg (2 half-lives), so \(t\) should be between 1590 and 3180 years: 2762 ✓.

Example 13.3 Cooling soup

A bowl of soup at 90°C is placed in a room kept at 25°C. After 10 minutes the soup is at 70°C. Assuming Newton's Law of Cooling, when will the soup reach 40°C?

(Similar to Stewart §6.5, Newton's Law of Cooling)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: \(T_0 = 90\), \(T_s = 25\), \(T(10) = 70\).   Find: \(t\) when \(T = 40\).

Plan: Work with the difference \(y = T - 25\), which decays exponentially. Find \(k\) from the 10-minute data, then solve for \(t\).

Step 1. Write the model for the difference from room temperature; \(y(0) = 90 - 25 = 65\):

\[T(t) - 25 = 65e^{kt}\]

Step 2. Use \(T(10) = 70\), so \(70 - 25 = 45\):

\[65e^{10k} = 45 \;\Longrightarrow\; e^{10k} = \frac{45}{65} = \frac{9}{13} \;\Longrightarrow\; k = \frac{\ln(9/13)}{10} \approx -0.03677\]

Step 3. Set \(T = 40\), so the difference is \(40 - 25 = 15\):

\[65e^{kt} = 15 \;\Longrightarrow\; e^{kt} = \frac{3}{13}\]

Step 4. Take \(\ln\) and solve for \(t\):

\[t = \frac{\ln(3/13)}{k} = \frac{10\ln(3/13)}{\ln(9/13)} \approx 39.9 \text{ min}\]

Answer: The soup reaches 40°C after about \(\boxed{39.9 \text{ minutes}}\). That is our answer.

Check: In the first 10 minutes the difference dropped from 65 to 45, a factor of about 0.69. To go from 65 to 15 is a factor of 0.23, which is about \(0.69^4\) (\(0.69^4 \approx 0.23\)), so about 40 minutes. ✓

Example 13.4 Continuous compounding

₱50,000 is deposited at 6% per year compounded continuously. (a) How much is in the account after 5 years? (b) How long until the deposit doubles?

(Similar to Stewart §6.5, continuously compounded interest)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: \(A_0 = 50\,000\), \(r = 0.06\), continuous compounding.   Find: (a) \(A(5)\); (b) \(t\) when \(A = 100\,000\).

Plan: Use \(A = A_0e^{rt}\). For (b), the doubling time does not depend on \(A_0\).

Step 1. Write the model:

\[A(t) = 50\,000e^{0.06t}\]

Step 2. (a) Substitute \(t = 5\):

\[A(5) = 50\,000e^{0.30} \approx 50\,000(1.349859) \approx 67\,492.94\]

Step 3. (b) Set \(A = 2A_0\) and cancel \(A_0\):

\[e^{0.06t} = 2 \;\Longrightarrow\; 0.06t = \ln 2 \;\Longrightarrow\; t = \frac{\ln 2}{0.06} \approx 11.55 \text{ years}\]

Answer: (a) about \(\boxed{\text{₱}67{,}492.94}\); (b) about \(\boxed{11.55 \text{ years}}\). That is our answer.

Check: The "rule of 70" estimates the doubling time as \(70/6 \approx 11.7\) years. ✓ And simple interest for 5 years would give only ₱65,000, so continuous compounding should give a bit more. ✓

Example 13.5 Inverse trig with a triangle

Find the exact value of (a) \(\sin^{-1}\!\left(-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)\) and (b) \(\tan\!\left(\sin^{-1}\tfrac13\right)\).

(Similar to Stewart §6.6, Examples 1 and 2)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: Inverse sine values.   Find: (a) an angle; (b) a trig value of an inverse-trig angle.

Plan: For (a), find the angle in \(\left[-\tfrac{\pi}{2}, \tfrac{\pi}{2}\right]\) with the given sine. For (b), draw the right triangle for \(\theta = \sin^{-1}\tfrac13\).

Step 1. (a) We need \(\theta\) with \(\sin\theta = -\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\) and \(-\tfrac{\pi}{2} \le \theta \le \tfrac{\pi}{2}\). Since \(\sin\tfrac{\pi}{3} = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\) and sine is odd:

\[\sin\!\left(-\tfrac{\pi}{3}\right) = -\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} \;\Longrightarrow\; \sin^{-1}\!\left(-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right) = -\tfrac{\pi}{3}\]

Step 2. (b) Let \(\theta = \sin^{-1}\tfrac13\), so \(\sin\theta = \tfrac13 = \tfrac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}}\). Draw a right triangle with opposite 1 and hypotenuse 3. By Pythagoras:

\[\text{adjacent} = \sqrt{3^2 - 1^2} = \sqrt8 = 2\sqrt2\]

Step 3. Read off the tangent (positive, since \(\theta\) is in the first quadrant):

\[\tan\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt2} = \frac{\sqrt2}{4}\]

Answer: (a) \(\boxed{-\tfrac{\pi}{3}}\); (b) \(\boxed{\tfrac{\sqrt2}{4}}\). That is our answer.

Check: (a) \(-\tfrac{\pi}{3}\) is in the allowed interval; \(\tfrac{4\pi}{3}\) also has sine \(-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\) but is outside it. (b) On a calculator: \(\sin^{-1}(1/3) \approx 0.3398\) and \(\tan(0.3398) \approx 0.3536 = \sqrt2/4\). ✓

Example 13.6 A derivative that simplifies, and a tangent line

Let \(f(x) = x\sin^{-1}x + \sqrt{1 - x^2}\) for \(-1 < x < 1\). (a) Find \(f'(x)\) and simplify. (b) Find the tangent line to \(y = f(x)\) at \(x = \tfrac12\).

(Similar to Stewart §6.6, derivative examples)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: \(f(x) = x\sin^{-1}x + \sqrt{1 - x^2}\).   Find: (a) \(f'(x)\); (b) the tangent line at \(x = \tfrac12\).

Plan: Product Rule on the first term, Chain Rule on the second. Then point-slope form for the tangent line.

Step 1. Product Rule on \(x\sin^{-1}x\):

\[\frac{d}{dx}\big(x\sin^{-1}x\big) = 1\cdot\sin^{-1}x + x\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}}\]

Step 2. Chain Rule on \((1 - x^2)^{1/2}\):

\[\frac{d}{dx}\sqrt{1 - x^2} = \frac12(1 - x^2)^{-1/2}(-2x) = -\frac{x}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}}\]

Step 3. Add; the two fractions cancel:

\[f'(x) = \sin^{-1}x + \frac{x}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}} - \frac{x}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}} = \sin^{-1}x\]

Step 4. (b) The point: \(f\!\left(\tfrac12\right) = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{\pi}{6} + \sqrt{\tfrac34} = \tfrac{\pi}{12} + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\). The slope: \(f'\!\left(\tfrac12\right) = \sin^{-1}\tfrac12 = \tfrac{\pi}{6}\). Point-slope form:

\[y - \left(\frac{\pi}{12} + \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right) = \frac{\pi}{6}\left(x - \frac12\right) \;\Longrightarrow\; y = \frac{\pi}{6}x + \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\]

Answer: (a) \(\boxed{f'(x) = \sin^{-1}x}\); (b) \(\boxed{y = \tfrac{\pi}{6}x + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}}\). That is our answer.

Check: At \(x = \tfrac12\) the line gives \(\tfrac{\pi}{12} + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} \approx 1.128\), equal to \(f(\tfrac12)\). ✓ (This also tells us that \(f\) is an antiderivative of \(\sin^{-1}x\), a fact you will meet again in Integral Calculus.)
