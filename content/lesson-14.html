6.7Hyperbolic Functions

Take a chain or a power cable and hold it at both ends. What shape does it hang in? It looks like a parabola, but it isn't. The curve is called a catenary (from the Latin word for chain), and its equation uses a new kind of function built from \(e^x\) and \(e^{-x}\). These are the hyperbolic functions. They behave so much like the trigonometric functions that they are named after them.

Definition of the hyperbolic functions \[\sinh x = \frac{e^x - e^{-x}}{2}, \qquad \cosh x = \frac{e^x + e^{-x}}{2}, \qquad \tanh x = \frac{\sinh x}{\cosh x} = \frac{e^x - e^{-x}}{e^x + e^{-x}},\] \[\operatorname{csch} x = \frac{1}{\sinh x}, \qquad \operatorname{sech} x = \frac{1}{\cosh x}, \qquad \coth x = \frac{\cosh x}{\sinh x}.\] We read them as "hyperbolic sine" ("sinch"), "hyperbolic cosine" ("cosh"), and so on.

Let's look at their graphs (Figure 14.1). Notice that \(\cosh x\) is even, has its lowest point at \((0, 1)\), and is always at least 1. \(\sinh x\) is odd and always increasing. \(\tanh x\) stays between \(-1\) and \(1\), and \(\tanh x \to \pm1\) as \(x \to \pm\infty\). A hanging cable has the shape \(y = c + a\cosh(x/a)\) (Figure 14.2).

Why "hyperbolic"? Recall that \(\cos^2 t + \sin^2 t = 1\), so \((\cos t, \sin t)\) moves around the circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\). For hyperbolic functions we have the identity

The fundamental identity \[\cosh^2 x - \sinh^2 x = 1.\] Proof: \(\cosh^2 x - \sinh^2 x = \dfrac{e^{2x} + 2 + e^{-2x}}{4} - \dfrac{e^{2x} - 2 + e^{-2x}}{4} = \dfrac{4}{4} = 1.\)

So the point \((\cosh t, \sinh t)\) moves along the right branch of the hyperbola \(x^2 - y^2 = 1\). Take note of the sign: it is a minus, not a plus. Dividing the identity by \(\cosh^2 x\) also gives \(1 - \tanh^2 x = \operatorname{sech}^2 x\).

Derivatives. These come straight from the definitions and \(\dfrac{d}{dx}e^{-x} = -e^{-x}\): \[\frac{d}{dx}\sinh x = \frac{e^x + e^{-x}}{2} = \cosh x, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\cosh x = \frac{e^x - e^{-x}}{2} = \sinh x.\] Notice the difference from trig: the derivative of \(\cosh x\) is \(+\sinh x\), with no minus sign. Using the Quotient Rule and the identity, \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\tanh x = \operatorname{sech}^2 x\). With the Chain Rule, for example, \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\cosh(x^2) = 2x\sinh(x^2)\).

Inverse hyperbolic functions. \(\sinh\) and \(\tanh\) are one-to-one, and \(\cosh\) is one-to-one on \([0, \infty)\), so (recall Module 11) they have inverses. Since the hyperbolic functions are made of exponentials, their inverses can be written with logarithms. For example, to solve \(y = \sinh^{-1}x\), we write \(\sinh y = x\), that is \(e^y - e^{-y} = 2x\). Multiply by \(e^y\): \((e^y)^2 - 2x\,e^y - 1 = 0\), a quadratic in \(e^y\). The quadratic formula gives \(e^y = x \pm \sqrt{x^2 + 1}\), and since \(e^y > 0\) we keep the plus sign:

Inverse hyperbolic functions and their derivatives \[\sinh^{-1}x = \ln\!\big(x + \sqrt{x^2 + 1}\big), \qquad \cosh^{-1}x = \ln\!\big(x + \sqrt{x^2 - 1}\big)\;(x \ge 1), \qquad \tanh^{-1}x = \tfrac12\ln\frac{1 + x}{1 - x}\;(|x| < 1),\] \[\frac{d}{dx}\sinh^{-1}x = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + x^2}}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\cosh^{-1}x = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2 - 1}}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\tanh^{-1}x = \frac{1}{1 - x^2}.\]

Compare these with the inverse trig derivatives from Module 13: \(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}}\) versus \(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1 + x^2}}\), and \(\dfrac{1}{1 + x^2}\) versus \(\dfrac{1}{1 - x^2}\). Only the signs change.

Common mistake Do not copy the trig signs. \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\cosh x = \sinh x\) (not \(-\sinh x\)), and \(\cosh^2 x - \sinh^2 x = 1\) (not \(+\)). Also, \(\cosh x \ge 1\) for every \(x\); it is not bounded between \(-1\) and 1 like cosine.
Key equations
\[ \sinh x = \frac{e^x - e^{-x}}{2}, \qquad \cosh x = \frac{e^x + e^{-x}}{2} \]
\[ \cosh^2 x - \sinh^2 x = 1 \]
\[ \frac{d}{dx}\sinh x = \cosh x, \quad \frac{d}{dx}\cosh x = \sinh x, \quad \frac{d}{dx}\tanh x = \operatorname{sech}^2 x \]

6.8Indeterminate Forms and l'Hospital's Rule

Let's go back to limits, where this whole course started. Suppose we want \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to1}\frac{\ln x}{x - 1}\). If we substitute \(x = 1\), we get \(\dfrac{\ln 1}{0} = \dfrac{0}{0}\). We have seen this before: \(\dfrac00\) does not mean the limit doesn't exist; it means the numerator and denominator are "racing" to zero and we need more information to see who wins. We call it an indeterminate form of type \(\tfrac00\). Back in Module 2 we removed such forms by factoring or multiplying by a conjugate. But there is no algebra that cancels \(\ln x\) with \(x - 1\). We need a new tool.

l'Hospital's Rule Suppose \(f\) and \(g\) are differentiable and \(g'(x) \ne 0\) near \(a\) (except possibly at \(a\)). If \[\lim_{x\to a}f(x) = 0 \text{ and } \lim_{x\to a}g(x) = 0 \qquad\text{or}\qquad \lim_{x\to a}f(x) = \pm\infty \text{ and } \lim_{x\to a}g(x) = \pm\infty,\] then \[\lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \lim_{x\to a}\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}\] if the limit on the right side exists (or is \(\pm\infty\)). The same holds for one-sided limits and for \(x \to \pm\infty\).

Why does it work? Near \(a\), both \(f\) and \(g\) are close to their tangent lines through \((a, 0)\): \(f(x) \approx f'(a)(x - a)\) and \(g(x) \approx g'(a)(x - a)\). So \(\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)} \approx \dfrac{f'(a)(x - a)}{g'(a)(x - a)} = \dfrac{f'(a)}{g'(a)}\). The ratio of the functions is controlled by the ratio of their slopes.

So for our example: \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to1}\frac{\ln x}{x - 1} = \lim_{x\to1}\frac{1/x}{1} = 1\) (Figure 14.3). And for an \(\tfrac{\infty}{\infty}\) form, \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{e^x}{x^2} = \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{e^x}{2x} = \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{e^x}{2} = \infty\). We used the rule twice, because after the first time it was still \(\tfrac{\infty}{\infty}\). This shows that \(e^x\) grows faster than any power of \(x\).

How to use l'Hospital's Rule (step by step).

  1. STEP 1: Substitute to identify the form. Only \(\tfrac00\) or \(\tfrac{\infty}{\infty}\) allows the rule.
  2. STEP 2: Differentiate the numerator and the denominator separately (this is not the Quotient Rule).
  3. STEP 3: Take the new limit. If it is again \(\tfrac00\) or \(\tfrac{\infty}{\infty}\), repeat; otherwise you are done.
Common mistake Always check the form first. For \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{1 + \cos x}{x^2 + 1}\), direct substitution gives \(\tfrac21 = 2\). If we wrongly apply l'Hospital's Rule we get \(\lim\frac{-\sin x}{2x} = -\tfrac12\), which is wrong. Also, never use the Quotient Rule here: it is \(f'/g'\), not \((f/g)'\).

Other indeterminate forms. The rule only works on quotients, so the other forms must first be turned into a quotient:

  • Products \(0\cdot\infty\): rewrite \(fg\) as \(\dfrac{f}{1/g}\) or \(\dfrac{g}{1/f}\). For example, \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0^+}x\ln x = \lim_{x\to0^+}\frac{\ln x}{1/x}\), of type \(\tfrac{-\infty}{\infty}\).
  • Differences \(\infty - \infty\): combine into one fraction with a common denominator.
  • Powers \(0^0\), \(\infty^0\), \(1^\infty\): take the natural logarithm. If \(y = f(x)^{g(x)}\), then \(\ln y = g(x)\ln f(x)\), which is a product. Find \(\lim\ln y = L\); then \(\lim y = e^L\). For example, \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\left(1 + \frac1x\right)^x = e\) (Figure 14.4), not 1.
Key equations
\[ \lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \lim_{x\to a}\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)} \quad\left(\text{type } \tfrac00 \text{ or } \tfrac{\infty}{\infty}\right) \]
\[ y = f^{g} \;\Longrightarrow\; \ln y = g\ln f, \qquad \lim y = e^{\lim\ln y} \]

★Key takeaways

  • sinh x = (eˣ − e⁻ˣ)/2 and cosh x = (eˣ + e⁻ˣ)/2; cosh²x − sinh²x = 1 (a hyperbola, not a circle).
  • d/dx sinh x = cosh x and d/dx cosh x = sinh x (no minus sign); d/dx tanh x = sech²x.
  • Inverse hyperbolic functions are logarithms, e.g. sinh⁻¹x = ln(x + √(x² + 1)), with derivative 1/√(1 + x²).
  • l'Hospital's Rule: for 0/0 or ∞/∞ only, lim f/g = lim f′/g′. Check the form before every use.
  • Turn 0·∞ and ∞ − ∞ into a single fraction; for 0⁰, ∞⁰ and 1^∞, take ln first and exponentiate at the end.

Example 14.1 From one hyperbolic value to the others

Suppose \(\sinh x = \tfrac34\). Find (a) \(\cosh x\), (b) \(\tanh x\), and (c) the number \(x\) itself, in exact form.

(Compare Stewart §6.7, identities and inverse functions)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: \(\sinh x = \tfrac34\).   Find: \(\cosh x\), \(\tanh x\) and \(x\).

Plan: Use the identity \(\cosh^2 x - \sinh^2 x = 1\) (remembering \(\cosh x \ge 1\)), then the definition of tanh, then the log formula for \(\sinh^{-1}\).

Step 1. Solve the identity for \(\cosh^2 x\):

\[\cosh^2 x = 1 + \sinh^2 x = 1 + \frac{9}{16} = \frac{25}{16}\]

Step 2. Take the positive root, because \(\cosh x\) is always positive:

\[\cosh x = \frac54\]

Step 3. Divide:

\[\tanh x = \frac{\sinh x}{\cosh x} = \frac{3/4}{5/4} = \frac35\]

Step 4. Use \(\sinh^{-1}x = \ln\big(x + \sqrt{x^2 + 1}\big)\); note \(\sqrt{x^2 + 1}\) here is just \(\cosh x\):

\[x = \sinh^{-1}\tfrac34 = \ln\!\left(\tfrac34 + \tfrac54\right) = \ln 2\]

Answer: (a) \(\boxed{\cosh x = \tfrac54}\); (b) \(\boxed{\tanh x = \tfrac35}\); (c) \(\boxed{x = \ln 2}\). That is our answer.

Check: Substitute \(x = \ln 2\) into the definitions, using \(e^{\ln 2} = 2\) and \(e^{-\ln 2} = \tfrac12\): \(\sinh(\ln 2) = \dfrac{2 - 1/2}{2} = \tfrac34\) ✓ and \(\cosh(\ln 2) = \dfrac{2 + 1/2}{2} = \tfrac54\) ✓.

Example 14.2 Differentiating a hyperbolic function

Differentiate \(y = x^2\cosh 3x\).

(Compare Stewart §6.7, derivatives of hyperbolic functions)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: \(y = x^2\cosh 3x\), a product.   Find: \(y'\).

Plan: Product Rule; the second factor needs the Chain Rule with \(\dfrac{d}{du}\cosh u = \sinh u\).

Step 1. Product Rule:

\[y' = \cosh 3x\,\frac{d}{dx}(x^2) + x^2\,\frac{d}{dx}(\cosh 3x)\]

Step 2. Chain Rule with \(u = 3x\), \(u' = 3\):

\[\frac{d}{dx}\cosh 3x = \sinh(3x)\cdot 3 = 3\sinh 3x\]

Step 3. Substitute and factor out \(x\):

\[y' = 2x\cosh 3x + 3x^2\sinh 3x = x\big(2\cosh 3x + 3x\sinh 3x\big)\]

Answer: \(\boxed{y' = 2x\cosh 3x + 3x^2\sinh 3x}\). That is our answer.

Check: Near \(x = 0\), \(\cosh 3x \approx 1\), so \(y \approx x^2\) and \(y' \approx 2x\); the formula gives \(2x(1) + 0\) at small \(x\). ✓ (A trig habit would give a wrong minus sign in front of \(3x^2\sinh 3x\).)

Example 14.3 An inverse hyperbolic function with the Chain Rule

Find the derivative of \(y = \sinh^{-1}(\tan x)\) for \(-\tfrac{\pi}{2} < x < \tfrac{\pi}{2}\), and simplify.

(Compare Stewart §6.7, derivatives of inverse hyperbolic functions)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: \(y = \sinh^{-1}(\tan x)\).   Find: \(y'\) in simplest form.

Plan: Chain Rule with \(\dfrac{d}{du}\sinh^{-1}u = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1 + u^2}}\) and \(u = \tan x\); then a trig identity.

Step 1. Let \(u = \tan x\), so \(u' = \sec^2 x\). Apply the Chain Rule:

\[y' = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + \tan^2 x}}\cdot\sec^2 x\]

Step 2. Use \(1 + \tan^2 x = \sec^2 x\):

\[y' = \frac{\sec^2 x}{\sqrt{\sec^2 x}} = \frac{\sec^2 x}{|\sec x|}\]

Step 3. On \(\left(-\tfrac{\pi}{2}, \tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)\), \(\cos x > 0\), so \(\sec x > 0\) and \(|\sec x| = \sec x\):

\[y' = \sec x\]

Answer: \(\boxed{y' = \sec x}\). That is our answer.

Check: At \(x = 0\): \(y' = \sec 0 = 1\). Near 0, \(\tan x \approx x\) and \(\sinh^{-1}x \approx x\), so \(y \approx x\), with slope 1. ✓

Example 14.4 Using l'Hospital's Rule three times

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{x - \sin x}{x^3}\).

(Similar to Stewart §6.8, Example 4)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: a quotient as \(x \to 0\).   Find: the limit.

Plan: Follow the three steps: identify the form, differentiate top and bottom separately, repeat while the form is still \(\tfrac00\).

Step 1. Substitute \(x = 0\): numerator \(0 - 0 = 0\), denominator \(0\). Type \(\tfrac00\), so l'Hospital's Rule applies:

\[\lim_{x\to0}\frac{x - \sin x}{x^3} = \lim_{x\to0}\frac{1 - \cos x}{3x^2}\]

Step 2. Substitute again: \(1 - \cos 0 = 0\) and \(3(0)^2 = 0\). Still \(\tfrac00\); apply the rule again:

\[= \lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin x}{6x}\]

Step 3. Still \(\tfrac00\). Apply the rule a third time (or recall \(\lim \frac{\sin x}{x} = 1\)):

\[= \lim_{x\to0}\frac{\cos x}{6} = \frac{\cos 0}{6} = \frac16\]

Answer: \(\boxed{\dfrac16}\). That is our answer.

Check: With a calculator at \(x = 0.01\): \(\dfrac{0.01 - \sin 0.01}{0.000001} \approx \dfrac{1.6667\times10^{-7}}{10^{-6}} \approx 0.1667\). ✓

Example 14.5 An indeterminate power

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0^+}(\cos x)^{1/x^2}\).

(Similar to Stewart §6.8, power examples)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: a power whose base \(\to 1\) and whose exponent \(\to \infty\).   Find: the limit.

Plan: Type \(1^\infty\): take ln to turn the power into a quotient, use l'Hospital's Rule, then exponentiate.

Step 1. Let \(y = (\cos x)^{1/x^2}\). Take \(\ln\) of both sides:

\[\ln y = \frac{1}{x^2}\ln(\cos x) = \frac{\ln(\cos x)}{x^2}\]

Step 2. As \(x \to 0^+\): \(\ln(\cos 0) = \ln 1 = 0\) and \(x^2 \to 0\). Type \(\tfrac00\); apply l'Hospital's Rule (\(\frac{d}{dx}\ln\cos x = \frac{-\sin x}{\cos x} = -\tan x\)):

\[\lim_{x\to0^+}\ln y = \lim_{x\to0^+}\frac{-\tan x}{2x}\]

Step 3. Still \(\tfrac00\). Apply the rule again:

\[= \lim_{x\to0^+}\frac{-\sec^2 x}{2} = -\frac12\]

Step 4. Exponentiate, since \(y = e^{\ln y}\) and \(e^x\) is continuous:

\[\lim_{x\to0^+}y = e^{-1/2}\]

Answer: \(\boxed{e^{-1/2} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt e} \approx 0.6065}\). That is our answer.

Check: At \(x = 0.01\): \((\cos 0.01)^{10000} = (0.99995)^{10000} \approx 0.6065\). ✓ The answer is not 1: the base is close to 1 but the exponent is huge.

Example 14.6 A product of type 0 · ∞

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0^+}x\ln x\).

(Similar to Stewart §6.8, Example 6)
Show solution (try it first)

Given: a product with \(x \to 0\) and \(\ln x \to -\infty\).   Find: the limit.

Plan: Rewrite the product as a quotient, \(\dfrac{\ln x}{1/x}\), to get type \(\tfrac{\infty}{\infty}\).

Step 1. Rewrite \(x = \dfrac{1}{1/x}\):

\[\lim_{x\to0^+}x\ln x = \lim_{x\to0^+}\frac{\ln x}{1/x}\]

Step 2. Numerator \(\to -\infty\), denominator \(\to \infty\): type \(\tfrac{\infty}{\infty}\). Apply l'Hospital's Rule:

\[= \lim_{x\to0^+}\frac{1/x}{-1/x^2}\]

Step 3. Simplify the compound fraction, then substitute:

\[= \lim_{x\to0^+}\left(\frac1x\cdot\frac{-x^2}{1}\right) = \lim_{x\to0^+}(-x) = 0\]

Answer: \(\boxed{0}\). That is our answer.

Check: At \(x = 0.001\): \(0.001\ln 0.001 \approx 0.001(-6.91) = -0.0069\), close to 0. ✓ The power \(x\) wins against the slow logarithm.
