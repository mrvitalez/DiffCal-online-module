6.7Hyperbolic Functions

Recall the exponential functions \(e^x\) and \(e^{-x}\). Certain combinations of them appear so often in mathematics and engineering that they have their own names. Stewart §6.7 calls them the hyperbolic functions: \[\sinh x = \frac{e^x - e^{-x}}{2}, \qquad \cosh x = \frac{e^x + e^{-x}}{2}, \qquad \tanh x = \frac{\sinh x}{\cosh x},\] together with \(\operatorname{csch} x = 1/\sinh x\), \(\operatorname{sech} x = 1/\cosh x\) and \(\coth x = \cosh x/\sinh x\). Notice that \(\sinh 0 = 0\), \(\cosh 0 = 1\), \(\cosh x \ge 1\) for every \(x\), \(\sinh\) is odd and \(\cosh\) is even, and \(\tanh x\) always lies between \(-1\) and \(1\), approaching \(\pm 1\) as \(x \to \pm\infty\).

Identities. Do these functions have an identity like \(\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x = 1\)? Yes. The most important one is \[\cosh^2 x - \sinh^2 x = 1.\] Let's see why. We square both: \(\cosh^2 x = \tfrac14\left(e^{2x} + 2 + e^{-2x}\right)\) and \(\sinh^2 x = \tfrac14\left(e^{2x} - 2 + e^{-2x}\right)\); subtracting leaves \(\tfrac44 = 1\). This is where the name comes from: the point \((\cosh t, \sinh t)\) lies on the hyperbola \(x^2 - y^2 = 1\), just as \((\cos t, \sin t)\) lies on the circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\). Dividing the identity by \(\cosh^2 x\) gives \(1 - \tanh^2 x = \operatorname{sech}^2 x\). There are also addition formulas, such as \(\sinh(x+y) = \sinh x\cosh y + \cosh x\sinh y\).

Derivatives. Using \((e^{-x})' = -e^{-x}\) we get \[\frac{d}{dx}\sinh x = \cosh x, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\cosh x = \sinh x, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\tanh x = \operatorname{sech}^2 x,\] and \((\operatorname{csch} x)' = -\operatorname{csch}x\coth x\), \((\operatorname{sech} x)' = -\operatorname{sech}x\tanh x\), \((\coth x)' = -\operatorname{csch}^2 x\). The Chain Rule works as usual: \(\frac{d}{dx}\cosh(4x) = 4\sinh(4x)\).

Common mistake Take note that trigonometry habits creep in. The derivative of \(\cosh x\) is \(+\sinh x\) (no minus sign), and the basic identity is \(\cosh^2 x - \sinh^2 x = 1\) (a minus sign, not a plus).

Inverse hyperbolic functions. \(\sinh\) and \(\tanh\) are one-to-one; \(\cosh\) is restricted to \([0,\infty)\). Because these functions are built from \(e^x\), their inverses can be written with logarithms. For \(y = \sinh^{-1}x\), let's write \(x = \frac{e^y - e^{-y}}{2}\) and multiply by \(2e^y\): \((e^y)^2 - 2x\,e^y - 1 = 0\). This is a quadratic in \(e^y\); since \(e^y \gt 0\), we keep the root \(e^y = x + \sqrt{x^2+1}\). In this way: \[\sinh^{-1}x = \ln\big(x + \sqrt{x^2+1}\big), \qquad \cosh^{-1}x = \ln\big(x + \sqrt{x^2-1}\big)\ (x \ge 1), \qquad \tanh^{-1}x = \tfrac12\ln\frac{1+x}{1-x}\ (|x| \lt 1).\] Their derivatives are \(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+x^2}}\), \(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x^2-1}}\) and \(\dfrac{1}{1-x^2}\). Let's compare with \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin^{-1}x = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\) and \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\tan^{-1}x = \dfrac{1}{1+x^2}\): only signs change.

The catenary. A flexible cable or chain hanging under its own weight between two supports takes the shape \(y = c + a\cosh(x/a)\), called a catenary (from the Latin word for chain). Have you noticed the power lines between poles? They are a familiar example. Near its lowest point a catenary looks like a parabola, but farther out it rises faster, because \(\cosh\) grows exponentially.

Key equations
\[ \sinh x = \frac{e^x - e^{-x}}{2}, \qquad \cosh x = \frac{e^x + e^{-x}}{2} \]
\[ \cosh^2 x - \sinh^2 x = 1 \]
\[ \frac{d}{dx}\sinh x = \cosh x, \quad \frac{d}{dx}\cosh x = \sinh x, \quad \frac{d}{dx}\tanh x = \operatorname{sech}^2 x \]
\[ \sinh^{-1}x = \ln\big(x + \sqrt{x^2+1}\big), \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\sinh^{-1}x = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+x^2}} \]
\[ \frac{d}{dx}\cosh^{-1}x = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2-1}}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\tanh^{-1}x = \frac{1}{1-x^2} \]

6.8Indeterminate Forms and l'Hospital's Rule

How do we find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 1}\frac{\ln x}{x - 1}\)? Top and bottom both approach 0, so the limit laws only give the meaningless \(0/0\). Such a limit is an indeterminate form of type \(\tfrac00\): it could turn out to be any number, or fail to exist. Similarly, \(\lim_{x\to\infty} \frac{\ln x}{x}\) is of type \(\tfrac{\infty}{\infty}\). Stewart §6.8 gives one powerful tool for both.

l'Hospital's Rule Suppose \(f\) and \(g\) are differentiable and \(g'(x) \ne 0\) near \(a\) (except possibly at \(a\)). If \(\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = 0\) and \(\lim_{x\to a} g(x) = 0\), or if both limits are \(\pm\infty\), then \[\lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \lim_{x\to a}\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)},\] provided the limit on the right exists (or is \(\pm\infty\)). The same is true for one-sided limits and for \(x \to \pm\infty\).

So, let's go back to our first limit: \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 1}\frac{\ln x}{x-1} = \lim_{x\to 1}\frac{1/x}{1} = 1\). What if the new quotient is still indeterminate? We use the rule again: \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{e^x}{x^2} = \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{e^x}{2x} = \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{e^x}{2} = \infty\). In the same way \(e^x\) beats every power of \(x\), and every power \(x^p\) with \(p \gt 0\) beats \(\ln x\): \(\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\ln x}{x^p} = 0\).

Cautions (1) Take note that we differentiate the top and the bottom separately; this is not the Quotient Rule. (2) We always check the form first. \(\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\cos x}{x + 1} = \frac11 = 1\) directly, but blindly "applying" the rule gives \(\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{-\sin x}{1} = 0\), which is wrong. (3) Sometimes the rule goes round in circles, and simple algebra works better.

Indeterminate products (\(0\cdot\infty\)). Let's rewrite \(fg\) as a quotient, \(\frac{f}{1/g}\) or \(\frac{g}{1/f}\). For example, \[\lim_{x\to 0^+} x\ln x = \lim_{x\to 0^+}\frac{\ln x}{1/x} = \lim_{x\to 0^+}\frac{1/x}{-1/x^2} = \lim_{x\to 0^+}(-x) = 0.\]

Indeterminate differences (\(\infty - \infty\)). We combine the terms into a single fraction (common denominator, rationalising or factoring) to reach \(\tfrac00\) or \(\tfrac{\infty}{\infty}\). For \(\lim_{x\to 0^+}\left(\frac{1}{x} - \frac{1}{\sin x}\right)\), write the difference as \(\frac{\sin x - x}{x\sin x}\), which is of type \(\tfrac00\).

Indeterminate powers (\(0^0\), \(\infty^0\), \(1^\infty\)). For \(y = f(x)^{g(x)}\), we take logarithms: \(\ln y = g(x)\ln f(x)\) is a product of type \(0\cdot\infty\). We find \(L = \lim \ln y\); then the original limit is \(e^{L}\). For \(\lim_{x\to 0^+}(1 + x)^{1/x}\) (type \(1^\infty\)): \(\ln y = \frac{\ln(1+x)}{x}\), and by the rule \(\lim_{x\to 0^+}\frac{1/(1+x)}{1} = 1\), so the limit is \(e^1 = e\).

Note Take note: do not stop at \(\lim \ln y = L\). The last step, \(\lim y = e^{L}\), is easy to forget. Also, \(1^\infty\) is not automatically 1: the base approaches 1 while the exponent grows, and the two effects compete.
Key equations
\[ \lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \lim_{x\to a}\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)} \quad \text{(type } \tfrac00 \text{ or } \tfrac{\infty}{\infty}\text{)} \]
\[ fg = \frac{f}{1/g} \quad \text{(type } 0\cdot\infty\text{)} \]
\[ y = f(x)^{g(x)}:\quad \ln y = g(x)\ln f(x), \qquad \lim y = e^{\lim \ln y} \]

★Key takeaways

  • sinh x = (eˣ − e⁻ˣ)/2 and cosh x = (eˣ + e⁻ˣ)/2 satisfy cosh²x − sinh²x = 1.
  • Take note that (sinh x)′ = cosh x and (cosh x)′ = sinh x, with no minus sign; (tanh x)′ = sech²x.
  • Inverse hyperbolic functions are logarithms, e.g. sinh⁻¹x = ln(x + √(x² + 1)), with derivatives 1/√(1 + x²), 1/√(x² − 1), 1/(1 − x²).
  • l'Hospital's Rule replaces f/g by f′/g′, but only for the forms 0/0 and ∞/∞, so we always check the form first.
  • We turn 0·∞ into a quotient, combine ∞ − ∞ into one fraction, and handle 1^∞, 0^0, ∞^0 by taking logarithms and finishing with e^L.

Example 14.1 From one hyperbolic value to the others

Suppose \(\sinh x = \tfrac34\). Find (a) \(\cosh x\), (b) \(\tanh x\), and (c) the number \(x\) itself, in exact form.

(Compare Stewart §6.7, identities and inverse functions)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • \(\sinh x = \tfrac34\).
  • Asked: (a) \(\cosh x\); (b) \(\tanh x\); (c) \(x\), exactly.
Step 1: Use the identity to find cosh²x

We solve \(\cosh^2 x - \sinh^2 x = 1\) for \(\cosh^2 x\) and substitute.

\[\cosh^2 x = 1 + \sinh^2 x\]

\[= 1 + \left(\tfrac34\right)^2 = 1 + \tfrac{9}{16}\]

\[= \tfrac{25}{16}\]

Step 2: Choose the correct square root (a)

\(\cosh x \ge 1\) for every \(x\), so we take the positive root.

\[\cosh x = \sqrt{\tfrac{25}{16}} = \tfrac54\]

Step 3: Find tanh x (b)

We use the definition \(\tanh x = \dfrac{\sinh x}{\cosh x}\).

\[\tanh x = \frac{3/4}{5/4} = \frac34\cdot\frac45 = \frac35\]

Step 4: Find x with the logarithm form of sinh⁻¹ (c)

We use \(\sinh^{-1}x = \ln\big(x + \sqrt{x^2 + 1}\big)\) with \(\tfrac34\) in place of \(x\).

\[x = \sinh^{-1}\tfrac34 = \ln\left(\tfrac34 + \sqrt{\tfrac{9}{16} + 1}\right)\]

\[= \ln\left(\tfrac34 + \sqrt{\tfrac{25}{16}}\right)\]

\[= \ln\left(\tfrac34 + \tfrac54\right)\]

\[= \ln 2\]

Answer

(a) \(\cosh x = \tfrac54\). (b) \(\tanh x = \tfrac35\). (c) \(x = \ln 2\).

Check

From the definitions, \(e^{\ln 2} = 2\) and \(e^{-\ln 2} = \tfrac12\), so \(\sinh(\ln 2) = \frac{2 - 1/2}{2} = \frac34\) ✓ and \(\cosh(\ln 2) = \frac{2 + 1/2}{2} = \frac54\) ✓. Take note that \(\cosh x\) can never be \(-\tfrac54\), unlike \(\cos x\), which can be negative.

Example 14.2 Differentiating an inverse hyperbolic function

Find the derivative of \(y = \sinh^{-1}(\tan x)\) for \(-\tfrac{\pi}{2} \lt x \lt \tfrac{\pi}{2}\), and simplify.

(Compare Stewart §6.7, derivatives of inverse hyperbolic functions)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • \(y = \sinh^{-1}(\tan x)\), \(-\tfrac{\pi}{2} \lt x \lt \tfrac{\pi}{2}\).
  • Asked: \(dy/dx\), simplified.
Step 1: Apply the Chain Rule

We use \(\dfrac{d}{du}\sinh^{-1}u = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1 + u^2}}\) with \(u = \tan x\), and multiply by the inner derivative \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\tan x = \sec^2 x\).

\[\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + \tan^2 x}}\cdot\sec^2 x\]

Step 2: Simplify with 1 + tan²x = sec²x

Recall the trigonometric identity \(1 + \tan^2 x = \sec^2 x\).

\[\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{\sec^2 x}{\sqrt{\sec^2 x}}\]

\[= \frac{\sec^2 x}{|\sec x|}\]

Step 3: Remove the absolute value

On \((-\pi/2, \pi/2)\) we have \(\cos x \gt 0\), so \(\sec x \gt 0\) and \(|\sec x| = \sec x\).

\[\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{\sec^2 x}{\sec x} = \sec x\]

Answer

So \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \sec x\).

Check

At \(x = 0\): \(y' = \sec 0 = 1\). Near 0 we have \(\tan x \approx x\) and \(\sinh^{-1}u \approx u\), so \(y \approx x\), and a slope of 1 makes sense ✓. Take note of a common slip: forgetting the inner derivative \(\sec^2 x\) gives \(1/\sqrt{1+\tan^2 x} = \cos x\) instead.

Example 14.3 Using l'Hospital's Rule several times, and an indeterminate power

Evaluate (a) \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{x - \sin x}{x^3}\) and (b) \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0^+}(\cos x)^{1/x^2}\).

(Similar to Stewart §6.8, quotient and power examples)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • (a) \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{x - \sin x}{x^3}\)
  • (b) \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0^+}(\cos x)^{1/x^2}\)
  • Asked: the value of each limit.
Step 1: Check the form of (a)

If we substitute \(x = 0\) right away, both top and bottom are 0.

\[\frac{0 - \sin 0}{0^3} = \frac{0}{0}\]

So the form is \(\tfrac00\) and l'Hospital's Rule applies.

Step 2: Apply the rule and check again

We differentiate the top and the bottom separately.

\[\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{x - \sin x}{x^3} = \lim_{x\to 0}\frac{1 - \cos x}{3x^2}\]

At \(x = 0\) this is \(\frac{1 - 1}{0} = \frac00\) again, so we keep going.

Step 3: Apply the rule two more times

Each time we check the form before going on.

\[\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{1 - \cos x}{3x^2} = \lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\sin x}{6x} \quad\left(\text{still } \tfrac00\right)\]

\[= \lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\cos x}{6}\]

\[= \frac{\cos 0}{6} = \frac16\]

Step 4: Take logarithms in (b)

The base \(\cos x \to 1\) and the exponent \(1/x^2 \to \infty\), so the form is \(1^\infty\). Let's write \(y = (\cos x)^{1/x^2}\) and take \(\ln\).

\[\ln y = \frac{1}{x^2}\ln(\cos x) = \frac{\ln(\cos x)}{x^2}\]

At \(x = 0\) this is \(\frac{\ln 1}{0} = \frac00\).

Step 5: Apply the rule to ln y

On top we need the Chain Rule: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln(\cos x) = \dfrac{1}{\cos x}\cdot(-\sin x)\).

\[\lim_{x\to 0^+}\frac{\ln(\cos x)}{x^2} = \lim_{x\to 0^+}\frac{-\sin x/\cos x}{2x}\]

\[= \lim_{x\to 0^+}\left(-\frac{\sin x}{x}\cdot\frac{1}{2\cos x}\right)\]

\[= -1\cdot\frac{1}{2(1)} = -\frac12\]

Step 6: Undo the logarithm

We found \(\lim \ln y = -\tfrac12\). Take note that the question asks for \(\lim y\), so we finish with \(e^L\).

\[\lim_{x\to 0^+}(\cos x)^{1/x^2} = e^{-1/2}\]

\[= \frac{1}{\sqrt e} \approx 0.607\]

Answer

(a) The limit is \(\dfrac16\). (b) The limit is \(e^{-1/2} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt e} \approx 0.607\).

Check

Numerically, at \(x = 0.01\): \((x - \sin x)/x^3 \approx 0.16666\) and \((\cos 0.01)^{10000} \approx 0.6065\). Both match ✓. In (b), the tempting answer \(1^\infty = 1\) is wrong. Do you know why? The base approaches 1 while the exponent grows, and the two effects compete.

Example 14.4 An indeterminate product

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty} x\sin\frac{3}{x}\).

(Similar to Stewart §6.8, indeterminate products)
Show solution (try it first)
Given
  • \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty} x\sin\frac{3}{x}\)
  • Asked: the value of the limit.
Step 1: Identify the form

As \(x \to \infty\), the first factor grows while \(\frac{3}{x} \to 0\), so \(\sin\frac3x \to \sin 0 = 0\).

\[x \to \infty, \qquad \sin\frac{3}{x} \to 0\]

The form is \(\infty\cdot 0\). We cannot use l'Hospital's Rule on a product directly.

Step 2: Rewrite the product as a quotient

We divide by the reciprocal of \(x\), which turns the product into the form \(\tfrac00\).

\[x\sin\frac{3}{x} = \frac{\sin(3/x)}{1/x}\]

Step 3: Differentiate the top and the bottom

On top we need the Chain Rule, with inner derivative \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\left(\dfrac3x\right) = -\dfrac{3}{x^2}\).

\[\frac{d}{dx}\sin\frac{3}{x} = \cos\frac{3}{x}\cdot\left(-\frac{3}{x^2}\right)\]

\[\frac{d}{dx}\,\frac{1}{x} = -\frac{1}{x^2}\]

Step 4: Simplify and take the limit

The factors \(-\frac{1}{x^2}\) cancel, and then we let \(x \to \infty\).

\[\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\sin(3/x)}{1/x} = \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\cos(3/x)\cdot(-3/x^2)}{-1/x^2}\]

\[= \lim_{x\to\infty}3\cos\frac{3}{x}\]

\[= 3\cos 0 = 3\]

Answer

So \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty} x\sin\frac{3}{x} = 3\).

Check

At \(x = 100\): \(100\sin(0.03) \approx 2.99955\), very close to 3 ✓. Take note that \(\infty\cdot 0\) is not automatically 0: here the two factors balance out at 3.
